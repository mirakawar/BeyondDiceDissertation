\abstract{
Deep learning models for mitochondria segmentation in electron microscopy (EM)
images are routinely evaluated using the Dice similarity coefficient, a
pixel-level overlap metric that is convenient but insensitive to biologically
significant failure modes including object merges, splits, and incorrect
organelle counts. This dissertation presents a standardised evaluation pipeline
comparing a custom U-Net and the self-configuring nnU-Net framework on the
CEM-MitoLab dataset (EMPIAR-11037), a heterogeneous collection of 21,871
annotated 2D EM images spanning multiple organisms, cell types, and imaging
modalities. Both models are trained and evaluated under identical data
conditions using a stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split at the
biological source level.

The dissertation is designed as a three-part critique of Dice as a sole
evaluation metric. It tests whether Dice compresses biologically important
performance differences, whether it fails to distinguish topologically different
error modes such as merges and splits, and whether apparently strong Dice scores
can still conceal biologically unreliable predictions. To do this, the study
combines voxel-level metrics with object-level metrics derived from the
instance-labelled ground truth, together with corner-case analysis, stratified
analysis. The main methodological
contribution is a reproducible evaluation framework that enables a controlled
comparison between a custom architecture and nnU-Net while exposing failure
modes that overlap metrics alone can conceal. On the corrected resplit test set,
nnU-Net outperforms the custom U-Net on most voxel-level and object-level
metrics (mean foreground Dice 0.686 vs.\ 0.636; mean PQ 0.551 vs.\ 0.480), but
both models still exhibit biologically relevant errors among apparently strong
predictions. Approximately 17\% of high-Dice predictions for each model still
show elevated object-level error, supporting the dissertation's central claim
that Dice alone is insufficient for biology-facing evaluation.
}

\chapter{Introduction}

Mitochondria are essential organelles that support cellular energy production,
apoptotic signalling, and metabolic regulation. Their morphology, including
size, shape, and network connectivity, is tightly linked to functional state.
Disruption of mitochondrial structure is implicated in a range of severe
diseases, including Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, diabetes, and
cancer \citep{SuomalainenNunnari2024Cell, tilokani2018mitochondrial}. Accurate,
large-scale quantification of mitochondrial morphology in biological tissue
therefore has direct relevance to both basic cell biology and translational
research.

Electron microscopy (EM) is the primary imaging modality for resolving
mitochondrial ultrastructure at nanometre resolution, revealing internal
organisation and spatial relationships that light microscopy cannot access.
However, EM datasets are large, heterogeneous, and labour-intensive to annotate
manually, which has driven the development of automated deep learning methods
for mitochondria segmentation \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. These methods
have advanced rapidly, from early patch-based convolutional networks to the
encoder-decoder U-Net architecture \citep{ronneberger2015unet} and, more
recently, the self-configuring nnU-Net framework \citep{isensee2021nnu}, which
automates hyperparameter selection from dataset properties alone.

\section{The Metric Gap Problem}

Despite this progress, a central problem in comparing and deploying
segmentation models remains unresolved. The standard evaluation metric, the
Dice similarity coefficient, measures pixel-level agreement between prediction
and ground truth, but it is largely insensitive to the spatial organisation of
individual objects. A model can achieve a high Dice score while still
fragmenting single mitochondria into multiple predicted components, merging
adjacent organelles into one, or substantially misestimating the total number
of mitochondria in a field of view. These are not minor technical
imperfections; they directly distort downstream biological analyses of organelle
count, size distribution, and network connectivity \citep{wei2020mitoem,
franco-barranco2022stable}.

The problem has three distinct aspects that motivate this dissertation's
evaluation framework. First, Dice \emph{compresses} the true performance gap
between models: two models that appear broadly similar on Dice can differ by an
order of magnitude on object-level metrics. Second, Dice is \emph{mode-blind}:
it cannot distinguish a model that merges objects from one that splits them, even
though these failure modes have opposite biological consequences. Third, Dice
provides \emph{false security}: a model achieving high Dice on an image may still
exhibit significant biological error that a biologist relying on the segmentation
would never detect without additional inspection.

\section{Contributions}

This dissertation makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}

\item A \textbf{standardised evaluation pipeline} ensuring both a custom U-Net
and nnU-Net are trained on identical data splits with identical preprocessing,
enabling fair direct comparison without confounds from data handling.

\item A \textbf{comprehensive metric suite} combining voxel-level metrics (Dice,
IoU, precision, recall, HD95, boundary F1, Expected Calibration Error) with
object-level biological metrics (object count error, merge rate, split rate,
Panoptic Quality) derived from the dataset's original instance annotations, all
computed per-image across the full held-out test set.

\item A \textbf{corner case analysis} that systematically identifies images on
which a model achieves high Dice ($>0.85$) yet exhibits elevated biological
error, characterising the distribution and nature of failures that pixel-level
evaluation conceals.

\item A \textbf{stratified analysis} by foreground density, showing how voxel
overlap and object-level quality vary as mitochondrial foreground becomes more
or less abundant in the image.

\item A \textbf{controlled experimental framework} for testing whether Dice
understates biologically meaningful performance differences, obscures distinct
failure modes, and overstates the reliability of apparently strong semantic
segmentations.

\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

Both models are trained to produce binary foreground/background semantic
predictions; instance labels from the dataset are used exclusively at evaluation
time to compute object-level metrics. The comparison is conducted on a single
dataset and results should be interpreted as specific to CEM-MitoLab's
conditions. nnU-Net is evaluated using a single training fold rather than its
default five-fold ensemble, which may underestimate its full potential.
Architectural and training differences between the two models, including batch
size, number of stages, and augmentation strategy, are acknowledged as
potential confounders and are discussed in Chapter~3.

\section{Report Structure}

Chapter~2 reviews relevant literature on EM segmentation methods, benchmark
datasets, and evaluation metrics. Chapter~3 describes the implementation,
covering data preparation, the data loading pipeline, training protocols for
both models, and the evaluation framework. Chapter~4 presents the experimental
results and analysis, covering both models individually and then comparing them
under the shared evaluation pipeline. Chapter~5 draws conclusions and discusses
limitations and future work.

\chapter{Background and Related Works}
This chapter reviews the literature on deep learning (DL) methods for the
automatic segmentation of mitochondria in electron microscopy (EM) images. The
high-resolution, volumetric nature of EM data makes manual annotation
labour-intensive and time-consuming, which in turn makes automated solutions a
practical necessity \citep{aswath2023segmentation}.

Mitochondria are central to cellular energy production, metabolism, and
regulation, and their morphology is closely linked to functional performance
\citep{SuomalainenNunnari2024Cell, tilokani2018mitochondrial}. Accurate
quantification of their size, geometry, and morphology is therefore important
for both basic neuroscience research and clinical studies of
neurodegeneration, metabolic disease, and cancer
\citep{SuomalainenNunnari2024Cell}.

The review covers the main developments in foundational architectures, benchmark
datasets, and more recent methods designed to address the challenges of
mitochondrial segmentation in EM images.

\section{Electron Microscopy}
Electron microscopy (EM) uses focused beams of electrons to image matter at
nanometre resolution, revealing ultrastructure that light microscopy cannot
resolve and enabling quantitative study of cells, organelles, and materials
\citep{aswath2023segmentation}. It is central to modern cell biology and
connectomics because it exposes the fine internal organisation and spatial
relationships that underpin cellular function and disease.

The two principal techniques are Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). In TEM, a beam of electrons is transmitted through an ultrathin specimen; regions of differing electron density produce contrast, allowing internal structures such as mitochondrial membranes and cristae to be resolved at sub-nanometre scale. SEM, by contrast, scans a focused beam across the specimen surface and collects backscattered or secondary electrons to produce high-resolution surface topography. In the context of volumetric EM (vEM), serial-section SEM approaches such as Focused Ion Beam SEM (FIB-SEM) and Serial Block-Face SEM (SBF-SEM) are widely used to reconstruct three-dimensional tissue volumes by repeatedly imaging and removing thin layers of a resin-embedded sample \citep{aswath2023segmentation}.

EM datasets are challenging because they are large, often anisotropic, and
packed with dense, visually similar structures under variable staining and
signal-to-noise conditions. They also vary substantially across tissues,
laboratories, and microscopes. Together, these factors make segmentation
methods sensitive to hand-tuned parameters and limit the generalisability of
models trained on a single dataset \citep{wei2020mitoem}.

\section{Foundational Deep Learning Architectures}
Deep learning methods have become the dominant approach to automated EM
segmentation because they can model complex non-linear relationships and scale
to large, heterogeneous datasets \citep{mathew2021deep, huang2025generalist,
aswath2023segmentation}.

\subsection{Convolutional Networks and the U-Net Paradigm}
Early deep learning approaches to segmentation used Fully Convolutional Networks
(FCNs), which replaced fully connected layers with convolutional layers to
enable dense pixel-wise prediction \citep{franco-barranco2022stable}. A major
breakthrough came with the U-Net architecture of \citet{ronneberger2015unet},
which extended the FCN encoder-decoder design by adding skip connections
between matching encoder and decoder stages. These skip connections preserve
high-resolution spatial detail while still allowing the network to exploit
coarse contextual features. Because U-Net performs strongly across biomedical
domains, particularly when labelled data are limited, it has become the de
facto baseline for later work in biomedical image segmentation
\citep{conrad2023instance, oktay2018attention}.

\subsection{Addressing Volumetric (3D) and Anisotropic Data}
Since mitochondrial morphology must often be studied in three dimensions, standard 2D approaches are extended or replaced by 3D network architectures, which are especially prominent in vEM workflows \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. The 3D U-Net proposed by \citet{cicek2016threeDunet} learns dense volumetric segmentation by operating directly on voxel volumes, capturing spatial relationships between adjacent slices that slice-by-slice 2D methods cannot exploit. However, many EM volumes are highly anisotropic, and the in-plane (x,y) resolution is substantially finer than the inter-slice (z) resolution, which limits the benefit of full 3D convolutions and motivates hybrid 2D--3D architectures that apply different operations along each axis \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. For 2D datasets such as CEM-MitoLab, where images are independent 2D slices rather than contiguous volumetric stacks, 2D architectures remain appropriate and avoid the additional computational overhead of volumetric convolutions.

\subsection{Self-Configuring Frameworks: nnU-Net}

A persistent limitation of custom U-Net variants is that their performance
depends heavily on manual design choices. Patch size, normalisation strategy,
loss function, learning rate schedule, and batch size must all be tuned to the
target dataset, which requires considerable domain expertise and iterative
experimentation. \citet{isensee2021nnu} addressed this directly by introducing
nnU-Net, a self-configuring segmentation framework that determines these
choices automatically through a process they term \textit{fingerprint
extraction}.

Given a new dataset, nnU-Net analyses properties such as image dimensions,
voxel spacing, intensity statistics, and class frequencies, and then applies a
fixed set of heuristic rules to configure an appropriate architecture (2D, 3D,
or a 3D cascade for highly anisotropic volumes), preprocessing pipeline, and
training schedule. No manual hyperparameter search is required. Despite this
automation, nnU-Net achieved state-of-the-art performance across 23 public
medical segmentation benchmarks, outperforming specialist methods that had been
manually tuned for individual tasks. This result shows that principled
automation can do more than substitute for expert configuration; it can also
surpass it \citep{isensee2021nnu}.

In the context of this dissertation, nnU-Net represents an important point of comparison: it is evaluated not only on predictive accuracy but on whether its automated configuration strategy yields results that are biologically reliable at the instance level, without the iterative tuning required by custom architectures.

\section{Segmentation Strategies}
DL-based segmentation methods generally fall into two categories: semantic
segmentation, which assigns a class to each pixel, and instance segmentation,
which separates individual objects. Instance segmentation is particularly
important for biological quantification, but it is challenging because
mitochondria frequently touch or overlap in EM images
\citep{aswath2023segmentation, conrad2023instance}.

\subsection{Supervised Semantic Segmentation}
Semantic segmentation assigns every pixel to the class `mitochondrion' or `background', producing a binary foreground mask \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. Early CNN-based approaches framed this as a patch classification problem: \citet{oztel2017mitochondria} trained a CNN to classify image patches and then applied a marker-controlled watershed transform and morphological filtering in post-processing, reporting strong performance on the Lucchi benchmark. Later work demonstrated that segmenting mitochondria simultaneously with other organelle classes can improve generalisation: \citet{heinrich2021wholecell} showed that a single 3D U-Net trained on 35 organelle classes from the OpenOrganelle collection outperformed specialist models, suggesting that greater training diversity acts as a regulariser.

A practical challenge in semantic segmentation of mitochondria is class imbalance: foreground pixels typically constitute a small fraction of each image, with background heavily dominating. Standard cross-entropy loss is biased toward the majority class under these conditions, causing models to underpredict mitochondria. Common mitigations include using Dice loss, which normalises by the predicted and ground-truth foreground volumes and is therefore inherently class-balanced, applying a positive-class weight to cross-entropy to penalise false negatives more heavily, or combining both into a compound loss function \citep{isensee2021nnu, franco-barranco2022stable}.

\subsection{Supervised Instance Segmentation}
The challenge of instance segmentation lies in successfully separating individual organelles, which is particularly difficult due to the frequent close contact and overlapping cross-sections of mitochondria in EM images \citep{wei2020mitoem}. Because bounding box scales are often undefined in EM volumes, bottom-up approaches, which predict masks or boundaries and then apply post-processing to recover instances, are typically preferred over top-down methods such as Mask R-CNN \citep{conrad2023instance}.

A widely adopted bottom-up strategy is boundary prediction followed by watershed segmentation. \citet{wei2020mitoem} proposed training a 3D U-Net to predict both binary foreground masks and contour maps (U3D-BC), then applying a marker-controlled watershed algorithm to separate touching instances (U3D-BC+MW). While effective on complex morphologies, this approach can fail when touching boundaries are ambiguous or when instances overlap extensively in cross-section.

To address these failure modes, shape-aware networks encode prior knowledge of mitochondrial geometry directly into the training objective. The Hierarchical Encoder-Decoder Network (HED-Net) decomposes the ground truth into complementary label maps based on the ovality of each mitochondrion, supervising the network with shape-derived targets rather than raw binary masks \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. Similarly, HIVE-Net incorporates a centreline-aware module to capture elongated shape priors \citep{chen2025gctransnet}. More recently, transformer-based architectures have been applied to this problem: GCTransNet replaces the U-Net encoder with a 3D Global Context Vision Transformer (GC-ViT) to model both long-range structural relationships and short-range spatial interactions, targeting small and discontinuous mitochondrial instances \citep{chen2025gctransnet}.

\section{Addressing Generalisation and Workflow Efficiency}
A major limitation of traditional DL methods is the failure to generalise when models are presented with unseen cellular contexts or different preparation/imaging protocols \citep{xie2025transfer}. This leads to expensive, repeated cycles of annotation and retraining \citep{conrad2023instance}.

\subsection{Generalist Deep Learning Models and Heterogeneous Data}
One response to the generalisation problem is to train models on very large,
diverse datasets. MitoNet, introduced by \citet{conrad2023instance}, is a
generalist instance segmentation model built on the Panoptic DeepLab
encoder-decoder architecture. Its encoder (ResNet50) was first pre-trained on
the unlabelled CEM1.5M dataset using self-supervised learning and then
fine-tuned for segmentation on CEM-MitoLab \citep{conrad2023instance}. This
training regime supports an important conclusion: sparsely sampled, noisy, but
heterogeneous labelled data can be more useful for training generalist models
than larger, homogeneous, expertly annotated datasets such as MitoEM
\citep{conrad2023instance}.

MitoNet performs well on established benchmarks and on newly introduced volumes
from diverse tissues, including mouse liver and kidney
\citep{xie2025transfer}. Even when inference is initially poor on a novel
dataset, the model can be refined quickly through fine-tuning on a small number
of annotated patches, which makes it a useful foundation for task-specific
adaptation. Its practical value is strengthened further by the
\texttt{empanada} Python package and napari plugin, which provide accessible
inference, visualisation, and interactive proofreading
\citep{conrad2023instance}.

\subsection{Transfer Learning}
Transfer learning initialises a network with weights pre-trained on a related
task or dataset before fine-tuning it on the target domain, rather than
training entirely from random initialisation \citep{xie2025transfer}. In the
context of vEM organelle segmentation, \citet{xie2025transfer} showed that
transfer learning can improve performance even when the source and target tasks
differ in both domain and objective, for example when transferring from mouse
brain neurite segmentation to rat liver mitochondria segmentation. Crucially,
combining transfer learning with fine-tuning on as few as 100 annotated patches
achieved an IoU of 92.37\% for mitochondria on a novel rat liver dataset,
outperforming MitoNet and SAM2 evaluated in zero-shot and prompted settings,
respectively \citep{xie2025transfer}. These results suggest that modest amounts
of target-domain annotation, when paired with a strong pre-trained
initialisation, can be more effective than training a generalist model from
scratch on a larger but less targeted dataset.

\subsection{Morphology-Function Prediction}

Beyond segmentation accuracy, recent work has begun to link mitochondrial
morphology directly to functional outcomes. \citet{ding2025mitochondrial}
demonstrate that morphological features extracted from segmentation masks can
predict mitochondrial membrane potential, respiration rate, and ATP generation
in live-cell fluorescence images. This points towards a future in which
segmentation quality is judged not only geometrically, but also by the
biological validity of the downstream inferences it supports.

\subsection{Interactive Segmentation and Uncertainty Analysis}
To reduce the extensive manual proofreading required after automated
segmentation, interactive DL frameworks allow users to refine predictions
iteratively through targeted feedback. \citet{jang2025deep} proposed a
Probabilistic Interactive (PI) segmentation framework that combines a
probabilistic U-Net with uncertainty analysis. Rather than producing a single
segmentation, the model produces a distribution over plausible segmentations
and highlights regions of high uncertainty as likely error-prone areas. The
user can then provide positive or negative clicks to correct those regions, and
the model updates its prediction accordingly. This uncertainty-driven feedback
mechanism achieved substantial efficiency gains, reducing overall analysis time
by approximately 90\%, specifically 90.3\% on Lucchi++ and 91.4\% on skeletal
muscle TEM data, while maintaining accuracy comparable to gold-standard manual
segmentation \citep{jang2025deep}.

\section{Evaluation Metrics for Segmentation}
\label{sec:bg-metrics}

The choice of evaluation metric is not a neutral decision; it determines which model behaviours are visible and which are hidden. This section reviews the metrics used to assess mitochondria segmentation, distinguishing between voxel-level overlap metrics and object-level metrics that capture biologically relevant failure modes.

\subsection{Voxel-Level Overlap Metrics}

The dominant metrics in the segmentation literature operate at the level of individual pixels or voxels. The \textit{Dice similarity coefficient} (DSC) measures the overlap between a predicted mask $P$ and a ground-truth mask $G$ as:
\[
\text{Dice} = \frac{2 |P \cap G|}{|P| + |G|}
\]
and ranges from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect agreement). \textit{Intersection over Union} (IoU, also called the Jaccard index) is a stricter variant:
\[
\text{IoU} = \frac{|P \cap G|}{|P \cup G|}
\]
Both metrics are widely used as the primary benchmark in mitochondria segmentation papers \citep{franco-barranco2022stable, wei2020mitoem, xie2025transfer} and are straightforward to compute from binary semantic masks.

Precision and Recall decompose the error into false positives (over-segmentation tendency) and false negatives (under-segmentation tendency) respectively, offering additional diagnostic value beyond the aggregate overlap that Dice reports. \textit{Hausdorff Distance at the 95th percentile} (HD95) measures the maximum surface distance between prediction and ground truth after discarding the worst 5\% of boundary errors, making it sensitive to large but localised deviations that Dice tolerates \citep{isensee2021nnu}.

\subsection{Limitations of Overlap Metrics}

Despite their prevalence, overlap metrics have well-documented limitations that are particularly acute in the context of mitochondrial morphology analysis. Most critically, Dice is a \textit{global} measure: a model can achieve high Dice by correctly classifying the large majority of background pixels while systematically merging adjacent mitochondria into single predicted objects, or fragmenting individual mitochondria into multiple disconnected components. In both cases, the count and morphology of individual instances are distorted, yet Dice may remain high \citep{wei2020mitoem, franco-barranco2022stable}.

This matters biologically because downstream analyses, such as estimating mitochondrial number density, volume distributions, or elongation ratios, depend entirely on the accuracy of instance-level segmentation. A model that achieves 0.90 Dice while generating systematic merge errors will produce systematically inflated volume estimates and deflated instance counts, leading to incorrect biological conclusions even when the pixel-level accuracy appears strong.

Furthermore, \citet{franco-barranco2022stable} note that performance on established benchmarks such as Lucchi has saturated, with multiple methods reporting near-identical Dice scores despite visibly different segmentation quality on complex morphologies. This motivates the use of additional metrics that probe different aspects of model behaviour.

\subsection{Object-Level and Biologically Motivated Metrics}

To complement overlap metrics, object-level metrics evaluate the model's behaviour at the level of individual instances. These metrics require instance-level ground truth in which each mitochondrion carries a unique identifier.

\textit{Merge rate} and \textit{split rate} directly quantify the two primary topological failure modes: a merge occurs when two or more ground-truth instances are fused into a single predicted object; a split occurs when a single ground-truth instance is fragmented into multiple predictions. These rates are diagnostic of specific boundary-detection failures and are more interpretable than Dice for assessing biological reliability \citep{wei2020mitoem}.

\textit{Panoptic Quality} (PQ), introduced by \citet{kirillov2019panoptic}, provides a unified instance segmentation metric that jointly accounts for detection and localisation quality:
\[
\text{PQ} = \underbrace{\frac{\sum_{(p,g) \in \text{TP}} \text{IoU}(p,g)}{|\text{TP}|}}_{\text{Segmentation Quality (SQ)}} \times \underbrace{\frac{|\text{TP}|}{|\text{TP}| + \frac{1}{2}|\text{FP}| + \frac{1}{2}|\text{FN}|}}_{\text{Detection Quality (DQ)}}
\]
PQ is zero for any predicted instance that does not match a ground-truth instance above a minimum IoU threshold (typically 0.5), making it substantially harder to inflate than pixel-level Dice.

Together, these object-level metrics form the foundation of a biologically motivated evaluation protocol: they assess not just whether the model segments the right \textit{pixels}, but whether it recovers the right \textit{objects} with the right \textit{boundaries}, which is ultimately what downstream biological quantification requires.

\section{Challenges and Future Outlook}
Despite major advances in both semantic and instance segmentation, several challenges remain that limit the practical reliability of automated mitochondria segmentation in EM images.

Generalisation across data heterogeneity is the most pervasive problem. Models trained on a specific tissue type or imaging protocol frequently fail when applied to images from different cellular contexts or preparation conditions, forcing expensive and time-consuming cycles of re-annotation and retraining \citep{xie2025transfer}. Continued curation of diverse, heterogeneous datasets such as CEM-MitoLab is therefore essential for training models that are robust across the range of conditions encountered in practice.

At the instance level, reliably separating tightly packed or morphologically complex mitochondria remains difficult. Structures such as mitochondria-on-a-string (MOAS), which exhibit thin connecting regions between swollen bodies, and mitochondria in close lateral contact, frequently produce merge and split errors even in models with high pixel-level accuracy \citep{wei2020mitoem}. This disconnect between overlap metrics and instance-level reliability is a central motivation for adopting biologically motivated evaluation protocols that make such failure modes visible.

Computational cost presents a further constraint: training deep 3D CNNs on large EM volumes demands significant GPU memory and time, limiting accessibility and the pace of iteration \citep{chen2025gctransnet, aswath2023segmentation}. Finally, reproducibility remains a structural problem in the field: many high-performing methods do not release code or report complete training details, making it difficult to verify or build upon published results \citep{franco-barranco2022stable}. The lack of standardised evaluation protocols compounds this issue by making cross-study comparison unreliable.

Looking ahead, the field is expected to shift from task-specific architectures toward general-purpose segmentation models that leverage large unlabelled datasets through self-supervised pre-training and adapt rapidly to specific tasks with minimal annotation \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. Interactive frameworks and accessible tooling such as \texttt{empanada} will be essential for bridging the gap between automated segmentation and the expert validation that biological research demands \citep{xie2025transfer}.






\chapter{Implementation}

This chapter describes the experimental pipeline used to compare the custom
U-Net and nnU-Net on CEM-MitoLab. The central design goal was to make the
comparison as controlled as possible: both models should train on the same
split, consume the same preprocessed inputs where feasible, and be evaluated by
the same post-processing and metric code.

\section{Overall System Design}

The pipeline has four components: dataset conversion and split definition, a
custom U-Net training stack, an nnU-Net training stack, and a shared
evaluation framework. The dataset contains 21,871 annotated 2D EM slices. In
the resplit workspace used for the final experiments, these are partitioned
into 15,312 training slices, 3,247 validation slices, and 3,312 test slices,
while preserving a source-level split of 415/89/89 biological samples across
train/validation/test. This separation matters because the dissertation is not
only comparing two segmentation models; it is also comparing them under a
controlled data regime intended to remove split-induced confounds.

The shared infrastructure around both models was written specifically for this
project: dataset conversion, split synchronisation, U-Net training and
inference, SLURM submission scripts, and the evaluation code. nnU-Net itself
was installed as an external package and treated as a black-box training
framework. The methodological effort therefore lay in making the surrounding
pipeline consistent and reproducible rather than in modifying nnU-Net's core
implementation.

\section{Data Preparation and Split Alignment}
\label{sec:impl-pipeline}

The raw CEM-MitoLab TIFF images and masks were converted into an nnU-Net-style
dataset named \texttt{Dataset001\_Mitochondria}. Semantic training masks were
derived from the original instance labels by binarising all non-zero
mitochondrion IDs, while the original instance masks were retained separately
for object-level evaluation. The conversion step also writes the custom fold
definition used throughout the project.

\subsection{Format Discovery: \texttt{.npz} to \texttt{.b2nd}}

nnU-Net v2.6 stores preprocessed training tensors as \texttt{.b2nd} files
rather than the older \texttt{.npz} format. The custom U-Net therefore reads
the same \texttt{.b2nd} tensors that nnU-Net trains on, using the
\texttt{blosc2} library to access the arrays on disk. This ensures that both
models see the same nnU-Net-preprocessed, z-score normalised image inputs
during training and validation.

\subsection{Variable Spatial Dimensions and Collation}

The preprocessed slices do not all share the same spatial dimensions, so
PyTorch's default batch collation is insufficient. To preserve the full field
of view, a custom \texttt{pad\_collate\_fn} pads each batch dynamically to the
maximum height and width present in that batch, and the dataset class pads each
sample to a multiple of 32 before it enters the network. This choice avoids any
crop-based asymmetry between the custom U-Net and nnU-Net while still allowing
mini-batch training.

\subsection{Split Alignment}

The canonical split file for the final experiments is
\texttt{splits\_final.json}, stored in both the raw and preprocessed nnU-Net
dataset directories. Fold~0 of this file is synchronised with the project's
\texttt{splits.json} and validated against \texttt{nnunet\_id\_map.json} before
training starts. The custom U-Net loader reads the same fold~0 definition, so
the U-Net and nnU-Net train on identical train/validation cases. The held-out
test set remains separate from this fold definition and is evaluated only after
training, using the same downstream evaluation script for both models.

\section{Custom U-Net}

The custom model is a 2D U-Net implemented in PyTorch from scratch. It follows
the standard encoder-decoder design with skip connections, using four
down-sampling blocks and a bottleneck, with channel widths 64, 128, 256, 512,
and 1024 before the decoder upsamples back to full resolution. Each
\texttt{DoubleConv} block uses two 3$\times$3 convolutions with
\texttt{InstanceNorm2d} and \texttt{ReLU} activations. Instance normalisation
was chosen because the custom model trains with a batch size of 4, for which
batch-normalisation statistics are unstable.

To make the comparison closer to nnU-Net, the U-Net now predicts two classes
(background and mitochondria) and is trained with a combined cross-entropy plus
soft Dice objective. The Dice term is implemented as batch-level softmax Dice
over the foreground channel only, matching nnU-Net's non-region
\texttt{CE + Dice} training path. During training and validation, the reported
Dice is also aligned with nnU-Net: it is computed from epoch-level hard
true-positive, false-positive, and false-negative counts rather than as a mean
of per-image Dice values.

The optimiser is \texttt{AdamW} with learning rate $10^{-3}$, and the learning
rate is reduced on plateau using \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} with patience 3 and
factor 0.5. Training is run for up to 1,000 epochs with validation every second
epoch and early stopping if validation loss fails to improve by at least 0.001
for 50 validation checks. Data augmentation is applied only to the training
split and consists of horizontal and vertical flips, random 90-degree
rotations, small brightness shifts, contrast scaling, and additive Gaussian
noise. The trainer writes both \texttt{best\_model.pt} and
\texttt{last\_checkpoint.pt}; the latter stores the model, optimiser,
scheduler, and loss history so that SLURM preemption does not force a restart
from scratch.

Training is launched through SLURM on the Hastings teaching partition with one
GPU, 32 GB RAM, and two CPU workers. Held-out test inference is performed in a
separate prediction step so that the final scoring of U-Net and nnU-Net is
always done through the same evaluation code path.

\section{nnU-Net Configuration}

nnU-Net was installed as a Python package and used without modification to its
own training logic. For this dataset it selected the 2D configuration, with a
patch size of 512$\times$512 and batch size 12. The resulting network is a
\texttt{PlainConvUNet} with eight stages, feature widths
\{32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 512, 512, 512\}, \texttt{InstanceNorm2d}
normalisation, and \texttt{LeakyReLU} activations. These choices come directly
from nnU-Net's planning stage and are part of what the dissertation is
evaluating: whether automatic configuration can compete with a custom
implementation under the same data split and evaluation framework.

Training is invoked as \texttt{nnUNetv2\_train 001 2d 0 --npz --c}. The
\texttt{--npz} flag preserves probability maps needed for calibration and
threshold-sensitivity analysis, while \texttt{--c} enables resume-from-checkpoint
behaviour under SLURM preemption. The launch script validates that the raw and
preprocessed \texttt{splits\_final.json} files match each other and match the
active project split before training begins, preventing nnU-Net from silently
falling back to its own autogenerated cross-validation folds.

A cluster-specific environment issue did require attention: under Python~3.12,
the local PyTorch 2.10.0+cu128 installation failed inside the inductor backend
because of an invalid type annotation in
\texttt{torch/\_inductor/codegen/cutedsl/cutedsl\_kernel.py}. This was resolved
by patching the PyTorch installation on the cluster, not by editing nnU-Net
itself. From the perspective of the dissertation, nnU-Net therefore remains an
unmodified external baseline.

\section{Evaluation Framework}

Evaluation is performed by a single script shared by both models. For each test
case, the script reads a binary prediction mask, a foreground probability map,
and the original instance-labelled ground truth. The same evaluation code is
used for U-Net and nnU-Net so that metric differences cannot be attributed to
post-processing differences.

\subsection{Voxel-Level Metrics}

Voxel-level metrics are computed at foreground thresholds
\{0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}. At each threshold the evaluation records
foreground Dice, background Dice, mean Dice, IoU, precision, recall, HD95, and
boundary F1 at 2-pixel and 5-pixel tolerance. HD95 is computed from boundary
pixels using Euclidean distance transforms rather than from the full mask.
Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is computed from the probability maps using 15
bins. This produces both a default-threshold comparison at 0.5 and a
threshold-sensitivity analysis showing whether the ranking between models is
stable across operating points.

\subsection{Object-Level Metrics}

Because both models are trained as semantic segmenters, predicted instances are
recovered by connected-component labelling of the binary foreground mask using a
3$\times$3 connectivity structure. These recovered components are compared with
the ground-truth instance masks by constructing a full pairwise IoU matrix and
then applying Hungarian matching for one-to-one assignments. From this matrix,
the evaluation computes object count error, signed count error, merge events,
split events, merge rate, split rate, connectivity error, mean matched IoU,
Panoptic Quality at IoU $> 0.5$, and object-level precision/recall at IoU
thresholds 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9. Merge and split events are derived from the
overlap structure itself: a predicted component overlapping multiple
ground-truth objects indicates a merge, whereas a ground-truth object overlapped
by multiple predicted components indicates a split.

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation with Instance Recovery}

Both models remain semantic segmentation systems throughout training; neither is
given an instance-aware loss or detector head. The object-level behaviour is
therefore assessed by applying the same connected-component post-processing to
both models at evaluation time. This is a deliberate methodological choice. The
question being asked is not whether either system is the best possible instance
segmentation model, but whether standard semantic segmentation outputs are
sufficiently biologically faithful once they are converted into object-level
predictions in the same way for both models.

\chapter{Results and Analysis}
\label{chap:results}

\section{Test-Set Overview}
\label{sec:test-overview}

The held-out test set contains 3,312 images drawn from biological sources not
seen during training. Of these, 2,694 (81.3\%) contain at least one
mitochondrion in the ground truth (\emph{GT-positive}), while the remaining 618
(18.7\%) are background-only slices. Because background-only images allow any
model to achieve trivially perfect background Dice and an object count of zero
by predicting nothing, the distinction between GT-positive and GT-empty cases is
maintained throughout the analysis. Unless otherwise stated, all aggregate
figures are reported over all 3,312 test images; supplementary GT-positive-only
figures are provided where the difference is diagnostically meaningful.

\subsection{Training Convergence}
\label{sec:nnunet-convergence}

nnU-Net trained for the full 1,000 epochs. The EMA pseudo-Dice on the
validation fold rose rapidly from 0.335 at epoch~1 to 0.749 by epoch~100,
after which improvement continued more slowly, with a sustained plateau through
the middle training phase. A renewed improvement phase began after epoch~800,
reaching a peak EMA pseudo-Dice of 0.855 at epoch~989 before declining
slightly to 0.833 at the final epoch~999. The \texttt{checkpoint\_best.pth}
saved at epoch~989 was used for all test-set inference, in preference to
\texttt{checkpoint\_final.pth}.

\subsection{Corner Case Analysis: High Dice Does Not Guarantee Biological Reliability}
\label{sec:nnunet-corner}

A central claim of this dissertation is that high Dice does not guarantee
biologically reliable predictions. To test this directly, the 1,684 test images
on which nnU-Net achieves foreground Dice $>0.85$ (50.8\% of the test set) are
examined for elevated object-level error. \emph{Elevated error} is defined as
count error $>4$ (the 75th percentile of $|$pred$-$gt$|$ over the full test
set) or split rate $>0$ (any split event present). Under this definition, 289
of the 1,684 high-Dice images (17.2\%) still exhibit at least one elevated
biological metric.

This rate is not negligible. A downstream biologist using Dice $>0.85$ as a
quality gate would accept approximately one in six predictions as reliable when
they are in fact topologically defective. The practical consequence is that
organelle counting or morphology analysis performed on this subset would include
a systematic contamination of incorrectly structured objects, even though the
pixel-level quality appears excellent.

The 75th-percentile thresholds for nnU-Net are notably strict (count error $>4$,
any split event), because the model is generally accurate and small deviations
from zero are biologically meaningful. This strictness reinforces the point: the
corner cases are not failures of a weak model but are genuine topological errors
in a strong one, invisible to the Dice filter.

\paragraph{Hausdorff Distance.}
HD95 is undefined on 438 images (13.2\%), slightly more than for nnU-Net. Over
the remaining 2,874 finite cases, the mean HD95 is 47.2\,px and the median is
18.5\,px. These values confirm that the U-Net not only has lower overlap than
nnU-Net, but is also less spatially precise at the boundary level.

\paragraph{High-Dice corner cases.}
The central claim of the dissertation is that high Dice does not guarantee
biological reliability. That claim is supported for both models. Using the same
simple rule as the earlier draft, a \emph{high-Dice corner case} is a slice
with foreground Dice $>0.85$ but either object count error $>4$ or split rate
$>0$. Under this definition, 289 of 1,684 high-Dice nnU-Net cases (17.2\%)
still show elevated object-level error. The U-Net behaves almost identically:
228 of 1,340 high-Dice slices (17.0\%) also fail this criterion. High overlap
therefore provides false reassurance for both architectures, even though
nnU-Net remains the better model overall.

\chapter{Conclusions}

\section{Summary of Findings}

This dissertation asked whether the Dice similarity coefficient is a sufficient
evaluation criterion for mitochondria segmentation in electron microscopy, using
a comparison between a custom U-Net and nnU-Net on CEM-MitoLab as the
experimental vehicle. Both models have now been fully evaluated on the held-out
test set under the corrected, split-aligned evaluation pipeline.

The results support a clear answer to the research question. nnU-Net achieves a
mean foreground Dice of 0.686 and mean PQ of 0.551, outperforming the custom
U-Net, which reaches 0.636 and 0.480 respectively. Yet strong Dice does not
imply biological reliability for either model. Around 17\% of high-Dice
predictions for both architectures still exhibit elevated object-level error
under the corner-case definition used in Chapter~4. Object count error, merge
and split rates, and Panoptic Quality therefore expose failure modes that
pixel-level overlap alone does not capture.

\section{Main Conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Dice similarity coefficient should not be treated as a sufficient
  standalone metric for a biology-facing mitochondria segmentation study,
  because it does not directly encode instance topology or object counting
  behaviour.

  \item A fair comparison between a custom U-Net and nnU-Net requires matched
  split handling, matched preprocessing where feasible, and a shared evaluation
  pipeline. Without that alignment, metric differences are difficult to
  interpret.

  \item Object-level analysis is necessary alongside voxel-level analysis when
  the downstream scientific question depends on organelle identity, count, and
  separation rather than foreground overlap alone.

  \item On the corrected resplit test set, nnU-Net is the stronger model
  overall. It exceeds the custom U-Net on voxel overlap, boundary quality, and
  object-level precision and recall, although the U-Net misses slightly fewer
  positive slices outright and under-counts objects somewhat less severely on
  average.

  \item High-Dice corner cases are not rare curiosities. Under a simple and
  conservative rule, 17.2\% of high-Dice nnU-Net slices and 17.0\% of
  high-Dice U-Net slices still show elevated object-level error.
\end{enumerate}

The central methodological contribution of this dissertation is a unified,
reproducible evaluation pipeline that makes the metric gap measurable and
interpretable, providing a framework for assessing biologically motivated
metrics alongside Dice in EM segmentation research.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations should be noted. Both models produce binary semantic
predictions; instance behaviour is recovered by connected-component
post-processing rather than instance-aware training, which may not reflect the
performance achievable by end-to-end instance segmentation architectures.
nnU-Net is evaluated using a single fold rather than its default five-fold
ensemble, likely underestimating its full capability. The U-Net was trained with
batch size 4 due to GPU memory constraints, compared to nnU-Net's batch size of
12, which may impede convergence independently of architecture. The comparison
is confined to one dataset; results should not be assumed to generalise beyond
the conditions represented in CEM-MitoLab. In addition, U-Net test inference
had to fall back to raw TIFF images with z-score normalisation because no test
\texttt{.b2nd} tensors were available, introducing a small mismatch between the
U-Net's training-time and test-time input path. Finally, no type-specific or
formal statistical significance analysis was completed for the final resplit
evaluation, so the comparison is descriptive rather than inferential.

Future work could evaluate nnU-Net with its full five-fold ensemble, compare the
current semantic models against genuinely instance-aware alternatives, apply the
evaluation framework to additional EM datasets and model families, and extend
the corner case analysis to 3D volumetric settings where merge and split errors
along the $z$-axis introduce additional complexity.
