\abstract{
Deep learning models for mitochondria segmentation in electron microscopy (EM)
images are routinely evaluated using the Dice similarity coefficient, a
pixel-level overlap metric that is convenient but insensitive to biologically
significant failure modes including object merges, splits, and incorrect
organelle counts. This dissertation presents a standardised evaluation pipeline
comparing a custom U-Net and the self-configuring nnU-Net framework on the
CEM-MitoLab dataset (EMPIAR-11037), a heterogeneous collection of 21,871
annotated 2D EM images spanning multiple organisms, cell types, and imaging
modalities. Both models are trained and evaluated under identical data
conditions using a stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split at the
biological source level.

The central contribution is a three-part empirical critique of Dice as a sole
evaluation metric. First, Dice compresses biologically critical performance
differences: foreground Dice is 0.312 for nnU-Net versus 0.191 for the U-Net,
yet object count error is 1.49 versus 15.03 objects per image and split rate is
0.053 versus 0.216. Second, Dice is blind to failure mode: both models produce
similar merge rates (${\approx}0.03$), yet U-Net fragments far more
ground-truth objects than nnU-Net --- a distinction invisible to pixel-level
metrics. Third, Dice provides false security: 167 of nnU-Net's 1,216 high-Dice
predictions (Dice $>$ 0.85) still exceed at least one object-level failure
cutoff. Paired Wilcoxon tests with Benjamini--Hochberg correction show 26 of 27
evaluated metric differences are statistically significant; recall at threshold
0.5 is the only non-significant comparison. Stratified analysis shows these
patterns hold across all foreground densities, object counts, and imaging types
present in the dataset.
}

\chapter{Introduction}

Mitochondria are essential organelles responsible for cellular energy production,
apoptotic signalling, and metabolic regulation. Their morphology --- size, shape,
and network connectivity --- is tightly coupled to functional state, and disruption
of mitochondrial structure is implicated in a range of severe diseases including
Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, diabetes, and cancer
\citep{SuomalainenNunnari2024Cell, tilokani2018mitochondrial}. Accurate,
large-scale quantification of mitochondrial morphology in biological tissue
therefore has direct relevance to both basic cell biology and translational
research.

Electron microscopy (EM) is the primary imaging modality for resolving
mitochondrial ultrastructure at nanometre resolution, revealing internal
organisation and spatial relationships that light microscopy cannot access.
However, EM datasets are large, heterogeneous, and labour-intensive to annotate
manually, motivating the development of automated deep learning methods for
mitochondria segmentation \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. Deep learning
approaches have advanced rapidly, from early patch-based convolutional networks
to the encoder-decoder U-Net architecture \citep{ronneberger2015unet} and the
self-configuring nnU-Net framework \citep{isensee2021nnu}, which automates all
hyperparameter decisions from dataset properties alone.

\section{The Metric Gap Problem}

Despite this progress, a fundamental problem in comparing and deploying
segmentation models remains largely unaddressed: the standard evaluation metric,
the Dice similarity coefficient, measures pixel-level agreement between
prediction and ground truth but is insensitive to the spatial organisation of
individual objects. A model that achieves a high Dice score may nonetheless
systematically fragment single mitochondria into multiple predicted components,
merge adjacent organelles into one, or substantially misestimate the total number
of mitochondria in a field of view. These are not minor technical imperfections
--- they directly corrupt downstream biological analyses of organelle count, size
distribution, and network connectivity \citep{wei2020mitoem,
franco-barranco2022stable}.

The problem has three distinct aspects that motivate this dissertation's
evaluation framework. First, Dice \emph{compresses} the true performance gap
between models: two models that appear broadly similar on Dice can differ by an
order of magnitude on object-level metrics. Second, Dice is \emph{mode-blind}:
it cannot distinguish a model that merges objects from one that splits them, even
though these failure modes have opposite biological consequences. Third, Dice
provides \emph{false security}: a model achieving high Dice on an image may still
exhibit significant biological error that a biologist relying on the segmentation
would never detect without additional inspection.

\section{Contributions}

This dissertation makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}

\item A \textbf{standardised evaluation pipeline} ensuring both a custom U-Net
and nnU-Net are trained on identical data splits with identical preprocessing,
enabling fair direct comparison without confounds from data handling.

\item A \textbf{comprehensive metric suite} combining voxel-level metrics (Dice,
IoU, precision, recall, HD95, boundary F1, Expected Calibration Error) with
object-level biological metrics (object count error, merge rate, split rate,
Panoptic Quality) derived from the dataset's original instance annotations, all
computed per-image across the full 5,677-image test set.

\item A \textbf{corner case analysis} that systematically identifies images on
which a model achieves high Dice ($>0.85$) yet exhibits elevated biological
error, characterising the distribution and nature of failures that pixel-level
evaluation conceals.

\item \textbf{Statistical validation} via paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with
Benjamini--Hochberg false discovery rate correction across all metrics, and
\textbf{stratified analysis} by foreground density, object count, and imaging
type, confirming that findings are consistent across the heterogeneous conditions
present in CEM-MitoLab.

\item An empirical demonstration that Dice understates the performance gap
between models by an order of magnitude, is blind to failure mode, and permits
high-confidence predictions that are biologically unreliable --- providing a
concrete, quantified case for why biologically motivated metrics are required
alongside overlap metrics in EM segmentation evaluation.

\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

Both models are trained to produce binary foreground/background semantic
predictions; instance labels from the dataset are used exclusively at evaluation
time to compute object-level metrics. The comparison is conducted on a single
dataset and results should be interpreted as specific to CEM-MitoLab's
conditions. nnU-Net is evaluated using a single training fold rather than its
default five-fold ensemble, which may underestimate its full potential.
Architectural and training differences between the two models --- including batch
size, number of stages, and augmentation strategy --- are acknowledged as
potential confounders and are discussed in Chapter~3.

\chapter{Background and Related Works}
This chapter explores the existing literature dedicated to leveraging deep learning (DL) techniques for the automatic segmentation of mitochondria in Electron Microscopy (EM) images. The high-resolution, volumetric nature of EM data necessitates automated solutions to overcome the labour-intensive and time-consuming manual annotation \citep{chen2025gctransnet}.

Mitochondria are essential organelles central to cellular energy production, metabolism, and regulation, and their morphology is fundamentally linked to functional performance \citep{jang2025deep}. Accurate quantification of their size, geometry, and morphology \citep{tilokani2018mitochondrial} is crucial for basic neuroscience research and clinical studies related to severe diseases, including bipolar disorder, diabetes, Parkinson's, Alzheimer's disease, and cancer \citep{wei2020mitoem, SuomalainenNunnari2024Cell}.

This literature review outlines the major developments in deep learning (DL) methodologies, foundational architectures, benchmark datasets, and advanced approaches developed to tackle the challenge of mitochondrial segmentation in EM images.

\section{Electron Microscopy}
Electron microscopy (EM) uses focused beams of electrons to image matter at nanometre resolution, revealing ultrastructure that light microscopy cannot resolve and enabling quantitative study of cells, organelles, and materials \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. It is central to modern cell biology and connectomics because it exposes the fine internal organisation and spatial relationships that underpin cellular function and disease.

The two principal techniques are Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). In TEM, a beam of electrons is transmitted through an ultrathin specimen; regions of differing electron density produce contrast, allowing internal structures such as mitochondrial membranes and cristae to be resolved at sub-nanometre scale. SEM, by contrast, scans a focused beam across the specimen surface and collects backscattered or secondary electrons to produce high-resolution surface topography. In the context of volumetric EM (vEM), serial-section SEM approaches such as Focused Ion Beam SEM (FIB-SEM) and Serial Block-Face SEM (SBF-SEM) are widely used to reconstruct three-dimensional tissue volumes by repeatedly imaging and removing thin layers of a resin-embedded sample \citep{aswath2023segmentation}.

EM datasets are challenging because they are large and often anisotropic, contain dense and visually similar structures with low signal-to-noise and variable staining, and vary considerably across tissues, laboratories, and microscopes. These factors make segmentation methods sensitive to hand-tuned parameters and limit the generalisability of models trained on a single dataset \citep{wei2020mitoem}.

\section{Foundational Deep Learning Architectures}
Deep learning methods, a subfield of machine learning utilizing artificial neural networks, have emerged as the dominant approach for automated segmentation in EM due to their ability to capture complex non-linear relationships and handle large, diverse datasets \citep{mathew2021deep, huang2025generalist, aswath2023segmentation}.

\subsection{Convolutional Networks and the U-Net Paradigm}
The first steps in DL for segmentation utilised Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs), which replaced fully connected layers with convolutional layers to enable dense, pixel-to-pixel prediction \citep{franco-barranco2022stable}. A major breakthrough came with the introduction of the U-Net architecture by \citet{ronneberger2015unet}, which extended the FCN encoder-decoder concept by adding skip connections between corresponding encoder and decoder stages. These skip connections allow high-resolution spatial detail captured by the encoder to be concatenated directly into the decoder's upsampling path, enabling precise pixel-wise classification without sacrificing contextual information. Due to its strong performance and generalisation capability across biomedical domains with limited training data, U-Net has since become the de facto baseline for virtually all subsequent work in biomedical image segmentation \citep{conrad2023instance, oktay2018attention}.

\subsection{Addressing Volumetric (3D) and Anisotropic Data}
Since mitochondrial morphology must often be studied in three dimensions, standard 2D approaches are extended or replaced by 3D network architectures, which are especially prominent in vEM workflows \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. The 3D U-Net proposed by \citet{cicek2016threeDunet} learns dense volumetric segmentation by operating directly on voxel volumes, capturing spatial relationships between adjacent slices that slice-by-slice 2D methods cannot exploit. However, many EM volumes are highly anisotropic — the in-plane (x,y) resolution is substantially finer than the inter-slice (z) resolution — which limits the benefit of full 3D convolutions and motivates hybrid 2D--3D architectures that apply different operations along each axis \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. For 2D datasets such as CEM-MitoLab, where images are independent 2D slices rather than contiguous volumetric stacks, 2D architectures remain appropriate and avoid the additional computational overhead of volumetric convolutions.

\subsection{Self-Configuring Frameworks: nnU-Net}

A persistent limitation of custom U-Net variants is that their performance depends heavily on manual decisions: patch size, normalisation strategy, loss function, learning rate schedule, and batch size must all be tuned to the target dataset, requiring considerable domain expertise and iterative experimentation. \citet{isensee2021nnu} addressed this directly by introducing nnU-Net, a self-configuring segmentation framework that automatically determines all such design choices through a process they term \textit{fingerprint extraction}.

Given any new dataset, nnU-Net analyses its properties — image dimensions, voxel spacing, intensity statistics, and class frequencies — and uses a fixed set of heuristic rules to configure an appropriate architecture (2D, 3D, or a 3D cascade for highly anisotropic volumes), preprocessing pipeline, and training schedule. No manual intervention or hyperparameter search is required. Despite this automation, nnU-Net achieved state-of-the-art performance across 23 public medical segmentation benchmarks, outperforming specialist methods that had been manually tuned specifically for each individual task — demonstrating that principled automation can not only substitute for expert configuration but surpass it \citep{isensee2021nnu}.

In the context of this dissertation, nnU-Net represents an important point of comparison: it is evaluated not only on predictive accuracy but on whether its automated configuration strategy yields results that are biologically reliable at the instance level, without the iterative tuning required by custom architectures.

\section{Segmentation Strategies}
DL-based segmentation efforts generally fall into two categories: semantic segmentation (pixel-wise classification) and instance segmentation (identifying individual instances). Instance segmentation is necessary for biological quantification and is challenging due to the frequent close contact or overlap of organelles \citep{aswath2023segmentation, conrad2023instance}.

\subsection{Supervised Semantic Segmentation}
Semantic segmentation assigns every pixel to the class `mitochondrion' or `background', producing a binary foreground mask \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. Early CNN-based approaches framed this as a patch classification problem: \citet{oztel2017mitochondria} trained a CNN to classify image patches and then applied a marker-controlled watershed transform and morphological filtering in post-processing, reporting an IoU of 0.907 on the Lucchi benchmark. % TODO: verify IoU 0.907 directly against Oztel et al. (2017) before submission Later work demonstrated that segmenting mitochondria simultaneously with other organelle classes can improve generalisation: \citet{heinrich2021wholecell} showed that a single 3D U-Net trained on 35 organelle classes from the OpenOrganelle collection outperformed specialist models, suggesting that greater training diversity acts as a regulariser.

A practical challenge in semantic segmentation of mitochondria is class imbalance: foreground pixels typically constitute a small fraction of each image, with background heavily dominating. Standard cross-entropy loss is biased toward the majority class under these conditions, causing models to underpredict mitochondria. Common mitigations include using Dice loss, which normalises by the predicted and ground-truth foreground volumes and is therefore inherently class-balanced, applying a positive-class weight to cross-entropy to penalise false negatives more heavily, or combining both into a compound loss function \citep{isensee2021nnu, franco-barranco2022stable}.

\subsection{Supervised Instance Segmentation}
The challenge of instance segmentation lies in successfully separating individual organelles, which is particularly difficult due to the frequent close contact and overlapping cross-sections of mitochondria in EM images \citep{wei2020mitoem}. Because bounding box scales are often undefined in EM volumes, bottom-up approaches — which predict masks or boundaries and then apply post-processing to recover instances — are typically preferred over top-down methods such as Mask R-CNN \citep{conrad2023instance}.

A widely adopted bottom-up strategy is boundary prediction followed by watershed segmentation. \citet{wei2020mitoem} proposed training a 3D U-Net to predict both binary foreground masks and contour maps (U3D-BC), then applying a marker-controlled watershed algorithm to separate touching instances (U3D-BC+MW). While effective on complex morphologies, this approach can fail when touching boundaries are ambiguous or when instances overlap extensively in cross-section.

To address these failure modes, shape-aware networks encode prior knowledge of mitochondrial geometry directly into the training objective. The Hierarchical Encoder-Decoder Network (HED-Net) decomposes the ground truth into complementary label maps based on the ovality of each mitochondrion, supervising the network with shape-derived targets rather than raw binary masks \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. Similarly, HIVE-Net incorporates a centerline-aware module to capture elongated shape priors \citep{chen2025gctransnet}. More recently, transformer-based architectures have been applied to this problem: GCTransNet replaces the U-Net encoder with a 3D Global Context Vision Transformer (GC-ViT) to model both long-range structural relationships and short-range spatial interactions, targeting small and discontinuous mitochondrial instances \citep{chen2025gctransnet}.

\section{Addressing Generalisation and Workflow Efficiency}
A major limitation of traditional DL methods is the failure to generalise when models are presented with unseen cellular contexts or different preparation/imaging protocols \citep{xie2025transfer}. This leads to expensive, repeated cycles of annotation and retraining \citep{conrad2023instance}.

\subsection{Generalist Deep Learning Models and Heterogeneous Data}
The generalisation problem is addressed by training models on massive, highly diverse datasets. MitoNet, introduced by \citet{conrad2023instance}, is a generalist instance segmentation model built on the Panoptic DeepLab encoder-decoder architecture. Its encoder (ResNet50) was first pre-trained on the unlabelled CEM1.5M dataset using self-supervised learning, then fine-tuned for segmentation on CEM-MitoLab \citep{xie2025transfer}. This training regime demonstrated an important principle: sparsely sampled, noisy but heterogeneous labelled data is more effective for training generalist models than larger, homogeneous, expertly annotated datasets such as MitoEM \citep{conrad2023instance}.

MitoNet performs well on established benchmarks and on newly introduced volumes from diverse tissues including mouse liver and kidney \citep{xie2025transfer}. Even when initial inference is poor on a novel dataset, the model can be rapidly refined through fine-tuning on a small number of annotated patches, demonstrating its value as a foundation for task-specific adaptation. Its utility is further enhanced by the \texttt{empanada} Python package and napari plugin, which provides accessible inference, visualisation, and interactive proofreading \citep{conrad2023instance}.

\subsection{Transfer Learning}
Transfer learning involves initialising a network with weights pre-trained on a related task or dataset and then fine-tuning on the target domain, rather than training from random initialisation \citep{xie2025transfer}. In the context of vEM organelle segmentation, \citet{xie2025transfer} demonstrated that transfer learning substantially improves performance even when the source and target tasks differ in both domain and objective — for instance, transferring weights learned on mouse brain neurite segmentation to rat liver mitochondria segmentation. Crucially, combining transfer learning with fine-tuning on as few as 100 annotated patches achieved an IoU of 92.37\% for mitochondria on a novel rat liver dataset, outperforming MitoNet and SAM2 evaluated in zero-shot and prompted settings respectively \citep{xie2025transfer}. These results suggest that modest amounts of target-domain annotation, when combined with a well-pre-trained initialisation, can be more effective than larger amounts of data used to train a generalist model from scratch.

\subsection{Morphology-Function Prediction}

Beyond segmentation accuracy, recent work has explored linking mitochondrial morphology directly to functional outcomes: \citet{ding2025mitochondrial} demonstrate that morphological features extracted from segmentation masks can predict mitochondrial membrane potential, respiration rate, and ATP generation in live-cell fluorescence images, pointing toward a future in which segmentation quality is evaluated not only geometrically but by the validity of the downstream biology it enables.

\subsection{Interactive Segmentation and Uncertainty Analysis}
To minimise the extensive manual proofreading required after automated segmentation, interactive DL frameworks have been developed that allow users to iteratively refine predictions with targeted feedback. \citet{jang2025deep} proposed a Probabilistic Interactive (PI) segmentation framework that combines a probabilistic U-Net with uncertainty analysis: the model produces not just a single prediction but a distribution over possible segmentations, and regions of high uncertainty are surfaced to the user as likely error-prone areas. The user can then provide positive or negative clicks to correct these regions, and the model updates its prediction accordingly. This uncertainty-driven feedback mechanism achieved substantial efficiency gains, reducing overall analysis time by approximately 90\% — specifically 90.3\% on Lucchi++ and 91.4\% on skeletal muscle TEM data — while maintaining accuracy comparable to gold-standard manual segmentation \citep{jang2025deep}.

\section{Evaluation Metrics for Segmentation}
\label{sec:bg-metrics}

The choice of evaluation metric is not a neutral decision --- it determines which model behaviours are visible and which are hidden. This section reviews the metrics used to assess mitochondria segmentation, distinguishing between voxel-level overlap metrics and object-level metrics that capture biologically relevant failure modes.

\subsection{Voxel-Level Overlap Metrics}

The dominant metrics in the segmentation literature operate at the level of individual pixels or voxels. The \textit{Dice similarity coefficient} (DSC) measures the overlap between a predicted mask $P$ and a ground-truth mask $G$ as:
\[
\text{Dice} = \frac{2 |P \cap G|}{|P| + |G|}
\]
and ranges from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect agreement). \textit{Intersection over Union} (IoU, also called the Jaccard index) is a stricter variant:
\[
\text{IoU} = \frac{|P \cap G|}{|P \cup G|}
\]
Both metrics are widely used as the primary benchmark in mitochondria segmentation papers \citep{franco-barranco2022stable, wei2020mitoem, xie2025transfer} and are straightforward to compute from binary semantic masks.

Precision and Recall decompose the error into false positives (over-segmentation tendency) and false negatives (under-segmentation tendency) respectively, offering additional diagnostic value beyond the aggregate overlap that Dice reports. \textit{Hausdorff Distance at the 95th percentile} (HD95) measures the maximum surface distance between prediction and ground truth after discarding the worst 5\% of boundary errors, making it sensitive to large but localised deviations that Dice tolerates \citep{isensee2021nnu}.

\subsection{Limitations of Overlap Metrics}

Despite their prevalence, overlap metrics have well-documented limitations that are particularly acute in the context of mitochondrial morphology analysis. Most critically, Dice is a \textit{global} measure: a model can achieve high Dice by correctly classifying the large majority of background pixels while systematically merging adjacent mitochondria into single predicted objects, or fragmenting individual mitochondria into multiple disconnected components. In both cases, the count and morphology of individual instances are distorted, yet Dice may remain high \citep{wei2020mitoem, franco-barranco2022stable}.

This matters biologically because downstream analyses --- such as estimating mitochondrial number density, volume distributions, or elongation ratios --- depend entirely on the accuracy of instance-level segmentation. A model that achieves 0.90 Dice while generating systematic merge errors will produce systematically inflated volume estimates and deflated instance counts, leading to incorrect biological conclusions even when the pixel-level accuracy appears strong.

Furthermore, \citet{franco-barranco2022stable} note that performance on established benchmarks such as Lucchi has saturated, with multiple methods reporting near-identical Dice scores despite visibly different segmentation quality on complex morphologies. This motivates the use of additional metrics that probe different aspects of model behaviour.

\subsection{Object-Level and Biologically Motivated Metrics}

To complement overlap metrics, object-level metrics evaluate the model's behaviour at the level of individual instances. These metrics require instance-level ground truth in which each mitochondrion carries a unique identifier.

\textit{Merge rate} and \textit{split rate} directly quantify the two primary topological failure modes: a merge occurs when two or more ground-truth instances are fused into a single predicted object; a split occurs when a single ground-truth instance is fragmented into multiple predictions. These rates are diagnostic of specific boundary-detection failures and are more interpretable than Dice for assessing biological reliability \citep{wei2020mitoem}.

\textit{Panoptic Quality} (PQ), introduced by \citet{kirillov2019panoptic}, provides a unified instance segmentation metric that jointly accounts for detection and localisation quality:
\[
\text{PQ} = \underbrace{\frac{\sum_{(p,g) \in \text{TP}} \text{IoU}(p,g)}{|\text{TP}|}}_{\text{Segmentation Quality (SQ)}} \times \underbrace{\frac{|\text{TP}|}{|\text{TP}| + \frac{1}{2}|\text{FP}| + \frac{1}{2}|\text{FN}|}}_{\text{Detection Quality (DQ)}}
\]
PQ is zero for any predicted instance that does not match a ground-truth instance above a minimum IoU threshold (typically 0.5), making it substantially harder to inflate than pixel-level Dice.

Together, these object-level metrics form the foundation of a biologically motivated evaluation protocol: they assess not just whether the model segments the right \textit{pixels}, but whether it recovers the right \textit{objects} with the right \textit{boundaries} --- which is ultimately what downstream biological quantification requires.

\section{Challenges and Future Outlook}
Despite major advances in both semantic and instance segmentation, several challenges remain that limit the practical reliability of automated mitochondria segmentation in EM images.

Generalisation across data heterogeneity is the most pervasive problem. Models trained on a specific tissue type or imaging protocol frequently fail when applied to images from different cellular contexts or preparation conditions, forcing expensive and time-consuming cycles of re-annotation and retraining \citep{xie2025transfer}. Continued curation of diverse, heterogeneous datasets such as CEM-MitoLab is therefore essential for training models that are robust across the range of conditions encountered in practice.

At the instance level, reliably separating tightly packed or morphologically complex mitochondria remains difficult. Structures such as mitochondria-on-a-string (MOAS), which exhibit thin connecting regions between swollen bodies, and mitochondria in close lateral contact, frequently produce merge and split errors even in models with high pixel-level accuracy \citep{wei2020mitoem}. This disconnect between overlap metrics and instance-level reliability is a central motivation for adopting biologically motivated evaluation protocols that make such failure modes visible.

Computational cost presents a further constraint: training deep 3D CNNs on large EM volumes demands significant GPU memory and time, limiting accessibility and the pace of iteration \citep{chen2025gctransnet, aswath2023segmentation}. Finally, reproducibility remains a structural problem in the field — many high-performing methods do not release code or report complete training details, making it difficult to verify or build upon published results \citep{franco-barranco2022stable}. The lack of standardised evaluation protocols compounds this issue by making cross-study comparison unreliable.

Looking ahead, the field is expected to shift from task-specific architectures toward general-purpose segmentation models that leverage large unlabelled datasets through self-supervised pre-training and adapt rapidly to specific tasks with minimal annotation \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. Interactive frameworks and accessible tooling such as \texttt{empanada} will be essential for bridging the gap between automated segmentation and the expert validation that biological research demands \citep{xie2025transfer}.






\chapter{Implementation}

This chapter discusses the design and construction of the experimental pipeline, covering the decisions taken, the difficulties encountered, and the degree to which each component was completed. Where relevant, the rationale behind technical choices is examined rather than the mechanics of individual code paths.

\section{Overall System Design}

The pipeline comprises four loosely coupled components: a data loading and preprocessing layer shared by both models, a custom U-Net training framework, an nnU-Net configuration and training setup, and an evaluation framework that computes both voxel-level and object-level metrics on a held-out test set. The separation of concerns was deliberate: by decoupling data preparation from model training, it was possible to guarantee that both models received identical inputs, which is a prerequisite for any fair comparison. Ensuring this equivalence turned out to be the most technically demanding aspect of the project and is discussed in detail in Section~\ref{sec:impl-pipeline}.

The custom U-Net training framework, the SLURM job submission scripts, and a shared \texttt{evaluate.py} applicable to both models were written from scratch. The nnU-Net framework itself was installed as a Python package without modification, and the \texttt{DiceCELoss} implementation was sourced from the \texttt{monai} library rather than reimplemented. Everything else — the U-Net architecture, the data loading pipeline, the augmentation logic, the evaluation metrics code, and the nnU-Net environment configuration — was built from scratch.

\section{Data Loading Pipeline}
\label{sec:impl-pipeline}

The data loading pipeline was the most technically challenging component of the project. The core requirement was straightforward: both models should train on the same preprocessed data using the same train/val/test split. Achieving this in practice required resolving three sequential problems, each of which only became visible after the previous one had been addressed.

\subsection{Format Discovery: \texttt{.npz} to \texttt{.b2nd}}

The initial design read nnU-Net's preprocessed data from \texttt{.npz} files stored under \texttt{nnUNet\_preprocessed/Dataset001\_Mitochondria/nnUNetPlans\_2d/}, using \texttt{nnunet\_id\_map.json} as a lookup table to map nnU-Net's internal integer sample IDs back to the original filenames. This approach failed immediately: nnU-Net v2.6 had silently changed its serialisation format from \texttt{.npz} to \texttt{.b2nd}, a blosc2-compressed array format. The change was not prominently documented in the version in use. Switching the reader to \texttt{.b2nd} via the \texttt{blosc2} library resolved the format issue.

\subsection{Variable Spatial Dimensions and Collation}

Reading \texttt{.b2nd} files correctly exposed a second problem: images in CEM-MitoLab have variable spatial dimensions. Individual samples range from 256$\times$384 to 512$\times$512 pixels, and PyTorch's default \texttt{collate\_fn} assumes that all items in a batch share the same shape so they can be stacked into a single tensor. Batches containing mixed-size images caused a shape mismatch error at runtime. Two solutions were considered: cropping all images to a fixed size, or padding each batch dynamically to the largest dimensions present in that batch. Cropping was rejected on methodological grounds — applying a fixed crop would introduce a preprocessing asymmetry between the U-Net (which would see cropped images) and nnU-Net (which had been configured on the full-resolution statistics), potentially invalidating the comparison. The adopted solution was a custom \texttt{pad\_collate\_fn} that zero-pads each image in a batch to the maximum height and width present in that batch before stacking. This preserves the full image content while satisfying PyTorch's batching requirements, and applies the same operation to both images and their corresponding masks.

\subsection{Split Alignment}

With the \texttt{.b2nd} reader and collation resolved, the final problem was aligning the U-Net's training split with the one nnU-Net had used for its own training. nnU-Net stores its cross-validation splits in \texttt{splits\_final.pkl}, which indexes samples by internal integer IDs rather than by filename. Using a different or independently generated split would mean the two models had trained on partially overlapping or entirely different data, making any comparison of their generalisation invalid. The fix was to load fold~0 from \texttt{splits\_final.pkl} and map the integer IDs back to original TIFF paths via \texttt{nnunet\_id\_map.json}, then pass these paths directly to the U-Net's \texttt{Dataset} class. This guaranteed that both models trained on identical samples and were evaluated on the same held-out test set.

The difficulty of this pipeline lay not in any single problem but in their sequential nature: each apparent fix revealed a new constraint that had been invisible until that point. The \texttt{.npz}$\to$\texttt{.b2nd} format change was an undocumented version-specific behaviour; the variable-size collation failure only appeared at runtime under real data; and the split alignment issue required understanding the internal data structures of nnU-Net's preprocessing pipeline. What unified all three was the same underlying challenge: ensuring that two models with entirely different codebases could be trained on a common, reproducible data view without either receiving a preprocessing advantage.

\section{Custom U-Net}

The U-Net architecture was implemented in PyTorch from scratch. The design follows the standard encoder-decoder structure with skip connections described in Section~3.2.1, instantiated as a 5-stage network with feature channels doubling from 32 at the first stage to 512 at the bottleneck. Instance Normalisation was chosen over Batch Normalisation because it is less sensitive to small batch sizes — with a batch size of 4, dictated by GPU memory constraints on the Hastings cluster, Batch Normalisation statistics are unreliable. Leaky ReLU activations were used throughout.

The loss function combined two terms: \texttt{DiceCELoss} (from \texttt{monai}), which sums Dice loss and cross-entropy with batch-level Dice computation, and Binary Cross-Entropy with a positive-class weight of 17.0766. This weight was not chosen arbitrarily — it was calculated exactly as the ratio of total background pixels to total foreground pixels across all training images, giving a principled correction for the foreground/background imbalance of approximately 6.2\%/93.8\%. The AdamW optimiser was used with a \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler that decreases the learning rate when validation Dice plateaus. Early stopping was applied with a patience of 50 epochs and a minimum improvement threshold of $\delta = 0.001$, up to a maximum of 1,000 epochs.

Training was submitted via SLURM to the \texttt{Teaching} partition on Hastings and completed with early stopping after 148 epochs in the requeued run. The best checkpoint was from epoch 48 (\texttt{val\_loss}=0.9348). Full U-Net test-set prediction and evaluation were then executed for all 5,677 held-out test images.

\section{nnU-Net Configuration}

nnU-Net was installed as a Python package and required no modifications to its core codebase. The configuration process — dataset fingerprinting, architecture selection, preprocessing, and training schedule — was entirely automatic, which is by design and constitutes part of the experimental comparison: the question of whether an auto-configured pipeline can match or exceed a hand-tuned one is central to the dissertation's argument.

Two problems arose during setup. The first was a PyTorch/Python incompatibility: PyTorch 2.10.0+cu128 contains a bug in which the inductor backend attempts to import \texttt{CSE[Any]} as a type annotation in \texttt{torch/\_inductor/codegen/cutedsl/cutedsl\_kernel.py}, which fails under Python~3.12's stricter typing enforcement. The crash occurred at import time before any training could begin. The fix was a one-line patch to line~77 of that file, replacing \texttt{Optional[CSE[Any]]} with \texttt{Optional[Any]}. This modification was applied to the PyTorch installation on the cluster only and did not alter nnU-Net's own code. An earlier attempt to resolve the issue by patching the nnU-Net trainer was reverted via \texttt{git checkout} once the root cause was identified in PyTorch.

The second problem was SLURM preemption: nnU-Net training was preempted by the job scheduler before completion. nnU-Net supports checkpoint resumption natively via the \texttt{--c} flag in its training command, which instructs it to resume from the latest saved checkpoint rather than restart. Once this flag was added to the SLURM script, any future preemption would resume automatically without manual intervention. Training ultimately ran for 969 epochs, reaching a best EMA Dice of 0.8992 on the validation set.

\section{Evaluation Framework}

The evaluation framework was written from scratch. No external library was used for the object-level metrics; the decision to implement them directly was partly pragmatic (avoiding dependency conflicts on the cluster) and partly to ensure full control over the exact matching logic, which needed to be consistent across both models.

\subsection{Voxel-Level Metrics}

The voxel-level metrics — Dice, IoU, Precision, Recall, HD95, Boundary F1 at 2px and 5px tolerance, ECE, and threshold sensitivity at five decision thresholds — are computed directly from binary prediction and ground-truth masks using \texttt{numpy} and \texttt{scipy}. These are largely routine to implement given the metric definitions; the main engineering consideration was ensuring that predictions are binarised at the correct threshold before each metric is computed, and that HD95 is calculated on boundary pixels rather than the full mask.

\subsection{Object-Level Metrics}

The object-level pipeline is more involved. Since the U-Net and nnU-Net both produce semantic (binary) predictions rather than instance-labelled outputs, predicted instances are recovered via connected-component labelling using \texttt{scipy.ndimage}. Ground-truth instance masks are already available with unique integer IDs per mitochondrion (stored alongside the semantic masks in the dataset). For each predicted component, the IoU is computed against every ground-truth instance it overlaps. This pairwise IoU matrix is then used to classify matches: a predicted component that overlaps multiple ground-truth instances above the IoU threshold constitutes a merge; a ground-truth instance overlapped by multiple predicted components constitutes a split; one-to-one matches above the threshold contribute to true positives. Merge rate, split rate, and object-level precision and recall at IoU thresholds of 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 are derived from these counts. Panoptic Quality is computed as the standard formulation: the product of mean IoU over matched pairs (Segmentation Quality) and the F1-equivalent detection score (Detection Quality), following \citet{kirillov2019panoptic}.

\subsection{Completion Status}

The evaluation framework was fully executed on both models' test-set predictions. For each model, per-image and aggregate outputs were generated over all 5,677 test cases. No cases were dropped due to missing binary masks or probability files in the final U-Net evaluation run.

ECE was also computed in the final pipeline because probability maps were available for both models (\texttt{.npz} files stored under each model's prediction directory).

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation with Instance Recovery}

It is worth stating explicitly that both the custom U-Net and nnU-Net are trained as \textit{semantic} segmentation models — they produce binary foreground/background masks, not instance-labelled outputs. The object-level metrics described above are therefore computed by applying connected-component labelling to the semantic predictions in post-processing, recovering pseudo-instances that are then matched against ground-truth instance masks. This is a deliberate pipeline choice rather than a limitation: the research question is not whether these models perform well as end-to-end instance segmentation systems, but whether their semantic outputs, when subjected to the same standard post-processing step, yield biologically reliable instance-level behaviour. Making this step explicit is important because it means any difference in object-level metrics between models reflects differences in their boundary prediction quality and foreground consistency, not differences in instance-aware training objectives.

\chapter{Results and Analysis}

This chapter presents the experimental results of evaluating the custom U-Net
and nnU-Net on the 5,677-image held-out test set. Results are organised to
address the three claims of the central hypothesis: that Dice compresses the
true performance gap, that Dice is blind to failure mode, and that Dice provides
false security via corner cases.

\section{Training Convergence}

The U-Net was trained on the Hastings compute cluster using a single NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 2080 Ti (11 GB). Training early-stopped after 148 epochs; the best
checkpoint was at epoch 48 (validation loss 0.9348, validation Dice 0.6234).
nnU-Net trained for 969 epochs and achieved a best exponential-moving-average
(EMA) validation Dice of 0.8992. Note that these validation Dice values are not directly comparable: the U-Net
metric is computed per full image and averaged, whereas nnU-Net reports an
exponential-moving-average over 256$\times$384 patch predictions on an
internal held-out subset.  Patch-based evaluation inflates Dice relative to
full-image evaluation, so the gap (0.623 vs.\ 0.899) reflects both genuine
model quality differences and methodological differences in measurement.  The
fair head-to-head comparison is on the shared test set using identical
\texttt{evaluate.py} code, reported in Chapter~4. Contributing
factors include nnU-Net's larger batch size (33 vs.\ 4, constrained by GPU
memory for full-image U-Net training), its extensive automatic augmentation
pipeline, and its polynomial learning rate decay which sustains gradient
updates throughout training compared to the U-Net's \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau}
scheduler.

\subsection{Dice compresses the true gap (Claim 1)}

Foreground Dice is 0.191 for U-Net versus 0.312 for nnU-Net, whereas the
object-level gap is much larger. U-Net predicts a mean of 20.16 objects per
image when the ground truth contains 5.88: a systematic over-prediction of
approximately 14 objects. nnU-Net predicts 6.04 objects per image, with signed
count error of only $+0.16$. Object count error is 15.03 for U-Net versus 1.49
for nnU-Net, a tenfold difference. Panoptic Quality is 0.102 versus 0.223, a
2.2-fold gap. These ratios are far larger than the overlap gap, confirming that
Dice understates how different the two models are at the biological task.

\subsection{Dice is blind to failure mode (Claim 2)}

The merge rates of both models are nearly identical: 0.032 (U-Net) versus 0.030
(nnU-Net), a difference of less than 1 percentage point. Yet split rates diverge
sharply: U-Net splits 21.6\% of ground-truth objects on average, nnU-Net only
5.3\% --- a fourfold difference. This means the U-Net's catastrophic object
count over-prediction is driven almost entirely by fragmentation, not fusion.
Dice is structurally unable to separate these two failure modes: a pixel
correctly placed on either side of a spurious boundary contributes identically
to the Dice numerator regardless of whether it is causing a merge or a split.
The object-level suite makes this distinction directly observable.

frequently fragmented by the U-Net. Both models perform best on crowded images
(Q4: 10--73 objects), where foreground signal is stronger: Dice rises to 0.241
for U-Net and 0.291 for nnU-Net. nnU-Net maintains a large object count
advantage across all object-count quartiles, with mean error between 1.34 and
2.24 compared with 13.46 to 20.18 for U-Net.

\chapter{Conclusions}

\section{Summary of Findings}

This dissertation asked whether the Dice similarity coefficient is a sufficient
evaluation criterion for mitochondria segmentation in electron microscopy, using
a comparison between a custom U-Net and nnU-Net on CEM-MitoLab as the
experimental vehicle. The results support a negative answer on three distinct
grounds.

\textbf{Dice compresses the true performance gap.} nnU-Net achieves a mean
foreground Dice of 0.312 versus the U-Net's 0.191 at threshold 0.5. The same
models differ tenfold on object count error (1.49 vs.\ 15.03), fourfold on
split rate (0.053 vs.\ 0.216), and 2.2-fold on Panoptic Quality (0.223
vs.\ 0.102). These ratios are far larger than the overlap gap, confirming that
Dice compresses biologically critical performance differences into a range that
appears smaller than their object-level consequences. An evaluator using Dice
alone would understate how much worse the U-Net is at preserving individual
mitochondrial identities.

\textbf{Dice is blind to failure mode.} Both models produce nearly identical
merge rates (0.032 vs.\ 0.030). Yet U-Net splits four times as many
ground-truth objects as nnU-Net. The U-Net's object count over-prediction ---
predicting 20.16 objects per image when 5.88 exist --- is driven almost entirely
by fragmentation. Dice cannot detect this asymmetry because it aggregates all
pixel-level errors without regard for spatial structure. The biological
consequences of merging (inflated organelle size, deflated count) and splitting
(deflated size, inflated count) are opposite and both scientifically damaging,
yet Dice treats both identically.

\textbf{Dice provides false security.} Even the better model, nnU-Net, achieves
Dice $> 0.85$ on 1,216 test images. Of those, 167 (13.7\%) still exhibit
elevated biological error under the corner case definition. High Dice is
therefore not a sufficient condition for biological reliability. A downstream
biologist screening for only high-Dice predictions would still include many
cases whose object-level topology is incorrect.

Twenty-six of 27 metric differences are statistically significant after
Benjamini--Hochberg correction; recall at threshold 0.5 is the only
non-significant comparison. All three findings replicate consistently across
foreground density quartiles, object count quartiles, and imaging type strata.

\section{Main Conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item nnU-Net is the substantially stronger segmentation model under this
  protocol: it achieves better overlap, boundary quality, calibration, and
  object-level accuracy on the held-out test set, and those advantages extend
  well beyond pixel-level accuracy into biological validity.

  \item Dice similarity coefficient, while correlated with true model quality,
  compresses an order-of-magnitude biological performance gap into an apparently
  marginal overlap difference, making it an unreliable sole criterion for
  comparing segmentation models in biological contexts.

  \item Object-level failure modes --- particularly splitting --- can dominate
  model behaviour while remaining largely invisible to voxel-level evaluation.
  High Dice does not imply correct instance topology.

  \item A robust evaluation protocol for EM mitochondria segmentation must
  include object-level metrics (object count error, merge and split rates,
  Panoptic Quality) and explicit corner case analysis alongside standard
  voxel-level metrics.
\end{enumerate}

The central methodological contribution of this dissertation is a unified,
reproducible evaluation pipeline that makes the metric gap measurable and
interpretable, providing a concrete empirical case for why biologically motivated
metrics are required alongside Dice in EM segmentation research.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations should be noted. Both models produce binary semantic
predictions; instance behaviour is recovered by connected-component
post-processing rather than instance-aware training, which may not reflect the
performance achievable by end-to-end instance segmentation architectures.
nnU-Net is evaluated using a single fold rather than its default five-fold
ensemble, likely underestimating its full capability. The U-Net was trained with
batch size 4 due to GPU memory constraints, compared to nnU-Net's batch size of
33, which may impede convergence independently of architecture. The comparison
is confined to one dataset; results should not be assumed to generalise beyond
the conditions represented in CEM-MitoLab.

Future work could add augmentation to the U-Net baseline to disentangle
architectural differences from training regime effects, evaluate nnU-Net with
its full five-fold ensemble, apply the evaluation framework to additional EM
datasets and model families, and extend the corner case analysis to 3D volumetric
settings where merge and split errors along the $z$-axis introduce additional
complexity.
