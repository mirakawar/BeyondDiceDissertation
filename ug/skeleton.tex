\abstract{
Deep learning models for mitochondria segmentation in electron microscopy (EM)
images are routinely evaluated using the Dice similarity coefficient, a
pixel-level overlap metric that is convenient but insensitive to biologically
significant failure modes such as object merges, splits, and incorrect
organelle counts. This dissertation presents a standardised evaluation pipeline
comparing a custom U-Net and the self-configuring nnU-Net framework on the
CEM-MitoLab dataset (EMPIAR-11037), a heterogeneous collection of 21,871
annotated 2D EM image slices spanning multiple organisms, cell types, and
imaging modalities. Both models are trained and evaluated under identical data
conditions using a stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split at the
biological source level.

The study tests three related claims: that Dice compresses biologically
important performance differences, that it fails to distinguish topologically
different error modes such as merges and splits, and that apparently strong Dice
scores can still conceal biologically important errors. To address this, the
evaluation combines voxel-level metrics with object-level metrics derived from
the instance-labelled ground truth, together with corner-case and stratified
analyses. The main methodological contribution is a reproducible evaluation
framework that enables a controlled comparison between a custom architecture and
nnU-Net while exposing failure modes that overlap metrics alone can conceal.
nnU-Net outperforms the custom U-Net on most
voxel-level and object-level metrics (mean foreground Dice 0.686 vs.\ 0.636;
mean PQ 0.551 vs.\ 0.480), but both models still exhibit biologically relevant
errors among apparently strong predictions. Under a simple rule-based
corner-case definition, 17.2\% of high-Dice nnU-Net predictions and 17.0\% of
high-Dice U-Net predictions still show elevated object-level error, supporting
the dissertation's central claim that Dice alone is insufficient for
biology-facing evaluation.
}

\chapter{Introduction}

Mitochondria are essential organelles that support cellular energy production,
cell survival, and metabolic regulation. Their morphology, including
size, shape, and overall organisation, is closely linked to cellular function.
Disruption of mitochondrial structure is implicated in a range of severe
diseases, including Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, diabetes, and
cancer \citep{SuomalainenNunnari2024Cell, tilokani2018mitochondrial}. Accurate,
large-scale quantification of mitochondrial morphology in biological tissue
therefore has direct relevance to both basic cell biology and translational
research.

Electron microscopy (EM) is widely used to observe
mitochondrial ultrastructure at nanometre scale, revealing internal
organisation and structural details that light microscopy cannot capture.
However, EM datasets are large, heterogeneous, and labour-intensive to annotate
manually, which has driven the development of automated deep learning methods
for mitochondria segmentation \citep{aswath2023segmentation}. These methods
have advanced rapidly, from early patch-based convolutional networks to the
encoder-decoder U-Net architecture \citep{ronneberger2015unet} and, more
recently, the self-configuring nnU-Net framework \citep{isensee2021nnu}, which
automates hyperparameter selection from dataset properties alone.

\section{Problem, Aim, and Research Question}

Despite this progress, a central problem in comparing and deploying
segmentation models remains unresolved. Standard voxel-overlap metrics such as
Dice and IoU measure pixel-level agreement between prediction and ground truth,
but they are largely insensitive to the spatial organisation of individual
objects. A model can achieve a high overlap score while still fragmenting a
single mitochondrion into multiple predicted components, merging adjacent
mitochondria into one, or misestimating the total number of
mitochondria in a field of view. Such errors directly distort downstream
biological analyses of organelle count, size distribution, and spatial
organisation \citep{wei2020mitoem, franco-barranco2022stable}.

This dissertation investigates whether standard voxel-overlap metrics are
sufficient for comparing segmentation models in a biology-facing setting. The
study uses a controlled comparison between a custom U-Net and nnU-Net on the
CEM-MitoLab electron microscopy dataset, and is organised around three aims.
First, it tests whether Dice can understate biologically meaningful differences
between models. Second, it examines whether Dice alone adequately reflects
distinct biologically relevant error patterns such as merges and splits. Third, 
it tests whether a model can achieve a high Dice score while still making errors 
that would matter in biological analysis.

This leads to the following research question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{To what extent are standard voxel-overlap metrics, particularly the Dice
similarity coefficient, sufficient for comparing a custom U-Net and nnU-Net on
mitochondria segmentation in electron microscopy, and which biologically
relevant failure modes do they fail to capture?}
\end{quote}

\section{Overview of Findings}

The final evaluation shows that nnU-Net is the stronger model overall on the
held-out test set, outperforming the custom U-Net across the main voxel-level,
boundary, calibration, and object-level metrics. At the default threshold,
nnU-Net achieves higher mean foreground Dice (0.686 vs.\ 0.636) and higher
Panoptic Quality (0.551 vs.\ 0.480), indicating that its advantage is not
limited to pixel overlap alone. Strong overlap scores also do not guarantee
biologically dependable predictions: under the rule-based corner-case
definition used later in the report, 17.2\% of high-Dice nnU-Net slices and
17.0\% of high-Dice U-Net slices still exhibit elevated object-level error.

\section{Contributions}

This dissertation makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}

\item A \textbf{standardised comparison pipeline} in which both a custom U-Net
and nnU-Net are trained on the same biological source-level data split and
evaluated with the same post-processing code, enabling a fairer
direct comparison.

\item A \textbf{biologically motivated evaluation framework} combining voxel-level metrics (Dice,
IoU, precision, recall, HD95, boundary F1, Expected Calibration Error) with
object-level biological metrics (object count error, merge rate, split rate,
Panoptic Quality) derived from the dataset's original instance annotations.

\item A \textbf{rule-based corner-case analysis} that identifies slices with
high Dice ($>0.85$) but elevated object-level error, showing that strong
overlap can still conceal biologically important failures.

\item A \textbf{stratified analysis} showing how model behaviour changes across
different image conditions, particularly mitochondrial foreground density.

\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background and Related Work}
Electron microscopy (EM) is valuable for mitochondria segmentation because it
provides the spatial detail needed to resolve organelle boundaries and internal
structure. That same detail also makes the task difficult. Mitochondria vary in
size, shape, contrast, and local context, and they often appear in close
contact with one another \citep{aswath2023segmentation, wei2020mitoem}. For
that reason, segmentation errors are often biologically meaningful rather than
minor: they appear as merges, splits, or incorrect counts.

A paper that reports high pixel overlap is not automatically solving the biologically relevant problem.
Foreground-background classification and instance reliability are related, but
not equivalent. A model may recover most mitochondria pixels correctly while
still distorting the number or shape of individual organelles. For this dissertation, 
the central question is whether methods that score well on benchmarks also produce 
segmentations that remain reliable for object-level biological analysis.

\section{Dataset Choice}

Smaller datasets such as Lucchi
and Lucchi++ have been useful for benchmarking, but they are now limited as
stress tests because performance has largely saturated and differences between
methods may not reflect behaviour on harder morphologies
\citep{lucchi2012supervoxel, franco-barranco2022stable, conrad2023instance}. Their main strength is
that they support clean, repeatable benchmarking with relatively stable labels.
Their main weakness is that they are too narrow to support strong claims about
robustness across imaging conditions or mitochondrial phenotypes. A method that
looks convincing on Lucchi-style data may simply not have been challenged very
hard.

MitoEM is much harder and remains important because it contains tightly packed
mitochondria and complex morphologies such as mitochondria-on-a-string
\citep{wei2020mitoem}. Its value is that it exposes failure modes hidden by
simpler benchmarks, especially failures of separation between adjacent
organelles. That makes it a better diagnostic dataset than Lucchi for this
dissertation's concerns. At the same time, MitoEM is still more narrowly defined
than CEM-MitoLab and is often used to motivate specialised instance-aware
pipelines. It therefore demonstrates the importance of the problem very well,
but is less well suited to asking whether two general-purpose semantic pipelines
remain reliable across a broader range of biological sources.

CEM-MitoLab is the most relevant dataset for this dissertation because it is
large and deliberately heterogeneous, combining material from many imaging
conditions, organisms, and biological sources \citep{xie2025transfer,
conrad2023instance}. This breadth is a strength because it makes the comparison
less dependent on a single narrow imaging regime. It also raises the standard
for evaluation: if a model performs well here, that result is harder to dismiss
as benchmark overfitting. The drawback is that heterogeneity also introduces
greater variation in label style, image quality, and local difficulty. This
means raw overlap scores become harder to interpret in isolation.

\section{Segmentation Approaches in the Literature}

The literature on mitochondria segmentation spans several model families. A
useful distinction is between semantic models, which assign pixels to
foreground or background, and methods that explicitly target instance
separation. Recent work also places greater emphasis on generalisation across
datasets and imaging conditions. This section introduces the main approaches
needed for the rest of the chapter.

\subsection{U-Net as the Standard Semantic Baseline}

U-Net, introduced by \citet{ronneberger2015unet}, is an encoder-decoder
convolutional network designed for dense biomedical image segmentation. The
contracting path extracts increasingly abstract features, while the expanding
path upsamples those features back to the original image resolution. Skip
connections link corresponding encoder and decoder stages so that fine spatial
detail is preserved during reconstruction. This combination of local detail and
global context is one reason U-Net became so influential in biomedical
segmentation.

For mitochondria segmentation, U-Net is especially relevant because EM images
contain thin boundaries, ambiguous textures, and tightly packed structures.
Later work often still compares against a U-Net variant even when proposing more
specialised methods \citep{conrad2023instance, oktay2018attention}. One major
strength of U-Net is therefore that it is clear, well understood, and easy to
adapt. At the same time, a reported "U-Net baseline" is often less standard than
it appears. Patch size, normalisation, augmentation, loss weighting, and
optimisation can all change performance substantially, so improvements over a
U-Net baseline sometimes reflect differences in training setup and tuning, not
just differences in model design.

There is also a task-specific limitation. Standard U-Net predicts a semantic
foreground mask rather than explicit object instances. Separate mitochondria can
be recovered afterwards with connected-component analysis or other
post-processing, but the network itself is not trained to preserve object
identity. Merge and split errors therefore remain an expected failure mode even
when pixel overlap is strong.

\subsection{nnU-Net as an Automated Baseline}

nnU-Net, introduced by \citet{isensee2021nnu}, is best understood as a
self-configuring segmentation framework rather than a single fixed model. It
starts from a dataset fingerprint, including image size, spacing, class balance,
and intensity statistics, and uses a set of heuristic rules to choose the
preprocessing pipeline, architecture configuration, patch size, training
schedule, and inference procedure. Depending on the dataset, this may lead to a
2D U-Net, a 3D U-Net, or a cascade.

The main strength of nnU-Net is standardisation. It reduces the number of manual
design choices that can quietly shape performance and has become a widely used
reference point in biomedical segmentation because it performs strongly across a
large range of tasks \citep{isensee2021nnu}. It is therefore important in the
current literature both as a practical tool and as an argument that a large
share of segmentation performance comes from sound engineering decisions rather
than from increasingly specialised architectures alone.

nnU-Net also has limitations. It can be computationally heavy, especially in
its full five-fold ensemble form, and it is still usually judged by benchmark
overlap scores. High semantic segmentation accuracy under that standard does not
necessarily mean that individual mitochondria are segmented in a way that
preserves correct counts, boundaries, or separation between neighbouring
objects.

\subsection{Instance-Aware Methods and What They Show}

A substantial part of the mitochondria segmentation literature moves beyond
binary semantic masks and targets instance separation directly. This is usually
done with bottom-up pipelines that predict masks, boundaries, or auxiliary shape
cues and then apply post-processing to recover individual instances
\citep{wei2020mitoem, conrad2023instance}. These methods matter here because
they show where semantic segmentation tends to fail when neighbouring
mitochondria have to be separated cleanly.

\citet{wei2020mitoem} make this point well through the U3D-BC and U3D-BC+MW
pipelines, which combine a boundary-aware 3D U-Net with
marker-controlled watershed post-processing. These models are effective because
they explicitly target touching mitochondria rather than treating all foreground
as one class. The strength of this approach is conceptual as well as empirical:
it aligns the modelling choice with the actual source of biological error. The
weakness is that it adds extra supervision targets and post-processing
dependencies, and its success still depends on the quality of predicted
boundaries. The main relevance of this work here is that it shows clearly that
separating individual mitochondria is a different problem from achieving strong
foreground overlap.

\citet{conrad2023instance} make a similar contribution through MitoNet, a
generalist instance segmentation model built on Panoptic-DeepLab and trained on
the diverse CEM-MitoLab dataset. The paper is important because it shows that
instance-labelled mitochondria data can support richer forms of analysis than
binary masks alone, and that broad training data can improve generalisation.
Its primary contribution, however, is resource creation and generalist instance
segmentation rather than a controlled comparison between semantic baselines.

Shape-aware and transformer-based methods push this further by encoding priors
about mitochondrial geometry or long-range structure
\citep{aswath2023segmentation, chen2025gctransnet}. Their advantage is that
they are designed for precisely the elongated, tightly packed, or fragmented
structures that cause merge and split errors. This is a real improvement over
plain semantic masking when the goal is instance fidelity. Their drawback is
that they are harder to compare fairly with simpler baselines, because they
often change the task formulation, supervision, dimensionality, and
computational cost at the same time.

\subsection{Recent Trends in the Literature}

Recent work has moved in two directions that are relevant for interpreting the
state of the field. One direction aims for broader generalisation. For example,
\citet{xie2025transfer} show that transfer-learning pipelines can improve
organelle segmentation across tissues, while \citet{huang2025generalist}
emphasise a generalist deep-learning segmentation tool for volume electron
microscopy. This line of work addresses a real weakness in older benchmark
culture: models that look strong on one curated dataset may not transfer well.
Its limitation is that it focuses more on generalisation and usability than on
how biological reliability should be evaluated.

A second direction is increasing task sophistication. Papers such as
\citet{jang2025deep} and \citet{chen2025gctransnet} continue to improve
segmentation quality through stronger architectures, richer context modelling,
or more specialised post-processing. \citet{jang2025deep} present an automated
mitochondrial segmentation pipeline for complex transmission electron microscopy images, while
\citet{chen2025gctransnet} introduce GCTransNet, a 3D transformer-based
pipeline. These methods make the literature harder to compare directly, because
task formulation, dimensionality, supervision, and computational cost often
change at the same time.

This point is reinforced by broader organelle-segmentation work such as
\citet{heinrich2021wholecell}, which shows that large-scale EM segmentation can
benefit from substantial annotation effort, specialised pipelines, and
application-specific engineering. That literature is valuable because it shows
how far segmentation systems can be pushed in well-resourced settings. It is
less useful as a direct template for this dissertation, because it often mixes
multiple organelles, 3D context, and highly customised workflows. These papers
are best read as context for the field rather than as direct baselines for a
mitochondria-specific comparison.

\section{Evaluation Metrics and Their Limits}
\label{sec:bg-metrics}

\subsection{Why Overlap Metrics Persist}

Dice and IoU dominate the segmentation literature because they are simple,
interpretable, and easy to compare across papers. Dice is especially popular in
biomedical segmentation because it is less harsh than IoU under class imbalance
and is often used directly as both a training objective and a reporting metric
\citep{franco-barranco2022stable, isensee2021nnu}. Precision, recall, and
boundary-sensitive measures such as HD95 provide additional detail about false
positives, false negatives, and local contour error.

For a predicted mask $P$ and ground-truth mask $G$, the Dice similarity
coefficient is defined as:
\[
\text{Dice} = \frac{2 |P \cap G|}{|P| + |G|}
\]
and the Intersection over Union (IoU) is:
\[
\text{IoU} = \frac{|P \cap G|}{|P \cup G|}
\]
Both range from 0 to 1, but IoU is stricter because it penalises disagreement
more heavily for the same prediction \citep{franco-barranco2022stable,
isensee2021nnu}. When written in terms of true positives (TP), false positives
(FP), and false negatives (FN), pixel-level precision and recall are:
\[
\text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}, \qquad
\text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}
\]
which makes clear that they separate over-prediction from under-prediction in a
way Dice alone does not \citep{franco-barranco2022stable, isensee2021nnu}.


These metrics persist for good reasons. They are easy to compute, widely
understood, and genuinely informative about foreground agreement. Within a fixed
evaluation setup, Dice is often the most convenient single summary for comparing
models at a glance, while IoU is
stricter and can penalise over-segmentation more heavily. Precision and recall
separate false-positive from false-negative behaviour in a way Dice does not,
and boundary-sensitive metrics can expose contour quality that overlap scores
blur. These metrics are useful, but they all describe agreement at the pixel
level. On their own, they do not show whether the model has recovered the
correct mitochondria as distinct objects.

Sometimes, comparison is complicated by inconsistent reporting. "Dice" may refer to
foreground Dice, mean Dice over classes, per-image averages, dataset-level
aggregates, or scores computed after different thresholding and post-processing
choices. Two papers can therefore both report Dice without reporting directly
comparable quantities.

This dissertation uses several voxel-level summaries, and each one answers a
slightly different question. Foreground Dice is the main overlap measure because
it reflects agreement on the mitochondria class directly. Background Dice is
usually easier to satisfy, especially in sparse images, so it is useful mainly
for checking whether performance is being inflated by the dominance of
background pixels. Mean Dice averages the two classes, which can be convenient
as a balanced summary but may still hide the fact that foreground quality is
the harder part of the task. IoU, precision, and recall provide a stricter and
more diagnostic view of the same predictions, especially by separating
over-prediction from under-prediction.

\subsection{Why Overlap Metrics Are Not Enough}

This limitation is not just theoretical. \citet{franco-barranco2022stable}
show that overlap-based benchmark scores can become very similar even when
segmentation quality still differs in visually and biologically important ways.
When that happens, small reported gains are harder to interpret as meaningful
practical improvement. \citet{wei2020mitoem} make a related point from the
instance-segmentation side: boundary-aware methods are needed because overlap
scores alone do not show whether touching mitochondria have been separated
correctly. Together, these papers suggest that overlap metrics can flatten
differences that matter for biological use.

For mitochondria segmentation, this matters because many downstream analyses are
instance-dependent. If a model merges neighbouring mitochondria, measured counts
decrease and object sizes inflate. If it splits one mitochondrion into many
parts, counts increase and morphology statistics become unreliable. These two
errors can have opposite biological implications while still producing broadly
similar overlap scores. Dice and IoU therefore answer only part of the question.
They tell us how well foreground pixels match, but not whether the model has
preserved the object structure that many biological analyses rely on. The
relevant criticism, then, is not that overlap metrics are useless, but that they
are incomplete for biology-facing evaluation.

The dissertation also examines threshold sensitivity by repeating foreground
Dice across several binarisation thresholds. This is useful because a model can
appear strong at the default threshold while depending on unusually fragile
probability calibration. A robust segmentation model should not change its
practical ranking dramatically under small threshold adjustments. This is not a
replacement for other metrics, but it helps distinguish models whose reported
overlap is stable from those whose apparent quality is threshold-dependent.

\subsection{Object-Level Metrics and What They Add}

Object-level metrics address this gap by evaluating predicted instances rather
than only predicted foreground pixels. Merge rate and split rate directly expose
the two topological error modes that matter most for this dissertation. Object
count error measures the practical consequence of those failures for
count-based analysis. Panoptic Quality (PQ) goes further by combining detection
and segmentation quality into a stricter instance-level summary
\citep{kirillov2019panoptic}.

In its standard form,
\[
\text{PQ} =
\underbrace{
\frac{\sum_{(p,g) \in \mathrm{TP}} \mathrm{IoU}(p,g)}{|\mathrm{TP}|}
}_{\text{Segmentation Quality (SQ)}}
\times
\underbrace{
\frac{|\mathrm{TP}|}{|\mathrm{TP}| + \frac{1}{2}|\mathrm{FP}| + \frac{1}{2}|\mathrm{FN}|}
}_{\text{Detection Quality (DQ)}}
\]
so it rewards not only accurate overlaps for matched instances, but also
penalises missing and spurious objects. This is why PQ is harder to inflate
than pixel-level Dice \citep{kirillov2019panoptic}.

Compared with Dice or IoU, these metrics ask a harder and more relevant
question: not just whether a segmentation overlaps the target, but whether it
recovers the right mitochondria as distinct biological objects. 
They are especially useful when two models appear similar on
overlap metrics but differ in how often they merge or fragment neighbouring
organelles. This makes them well suited to the central comparison in this
dissertation.

Some of the object metrics used here are more diagnostic than others. Matched
instance IoU measures how well the predicted objects that do correspond to real
mitochondria overlap their targets once matching has been established. It is
useful because it separates the quality of matched objects from the problem of
missing or extra objects. Object precision and object recall at multiple IoU
thresholds make this stricter or looser in a controlled way: low thresholds ask
whether the right objects were found at all, while high thresholds ask whether
they were recovered accurately enough to support closer biological inspection.
These thresholded object measures are less common in mitochondria papers than
Dice, but they are helpful here because they reveal whether one model's apparent
advantage depends on forgiving or stringent matching.

These metrics also have limitations. Count error is easy to interpret, but it
does not explain why the count is wrong. Merge and split rates are highly
diagnostic, but less standardised across papers than Dice or IoU. PQ is harder
to inflate than Dice, but it still depends on a matching threshold and
compresses multiple behaviours into one number. The implication is not that any
single object metric solves evaluation on its own. Rather, object-level metrics
provide the missing evidence needed to interpret whether strong semantic scores
actually correspond to biologically reliable predictions.

\subsection{Boundary and Calibration Metrics}

Between pure overlap metrics and pure object-level metrics, this dissertation
also uses two intermediate families of measures: boundary quality and
calibration. These are less common in mitochondria segmentation papers than
Dice, but they are useful because they expose different weaknesses in model
behaviour.

HD95 summarises the 95th percentile Hausdorff distance between predicted and
ground-truth boundaries. Its strength is that it detects large local contour
errors that overlap metrics may blur, especially around thin protrusions or
misshapen borders. Its weakness is that it can be unstable on extreme failure
cases and is sensitive to outlier geometry, which is why the 95th percentile is
preferred over the raw Hausdorff maximum. Boundary F1 at fixed tolerances
provides a more directly interpretable alternative: it asks what proportion of
predicted and true boundary pixels fall within a small tolerance band. Using two
tolerances, 2px and 5px, gives a stricter and more forgiving view of contour
quality. These metrics are still not object-aware, but they are more sensitive
to boundary fidelity than Dice or IoU alone.

Expected Calibration Error (ECE) addresses a different issue. A segmentation
model does not only output hard masks; it also produces confidence values.
Calibration asks whether those confidence values are trustworthy. A model is
poorly calibrated if it is systematically overconfident or underconfident,
regardless of whether its hard predictions are good. This matters here because
threshold sensitivity and confidence reliability both affect how easy a model is
to use in practice. A method that looks strong on Dice but produces poorly
calibrated probabilities may be less dependable when decision thresholds change
or when a biologist tries to interpret uncertainty.

\subsection{The Full Metric Set Used in This Dissertation}

The final evaluation therefore combines several metric families rather than
relying on a single headline score. At the voxel level, it reports foreground
Dice, background Dice, mean Dice, IoU, precision, and recall. It also tests
foreground Dice across multiple probability thresholds to assess threshold
stability. At the boundary level, it reports HD95 and boundary F1 at 2-pixel
and 5-pixel tolerances. At the calibration level, it reports ECE. At the
object level, it reports object count error, merge rate, split rate, matched
instance IoU, object precision and recall at multiple IoU thresholds, and PQ.
These are not redundant diagnostics. Together they separate semantic overlap,
contour quality, confidence reliability, object recovery, and object
separation.

Each metric family is included for a reason. Overlap metrics summarise semantic
agreement. Boundary metrics test contour quality. Calibration tests confidence
reliability. Object-level metrics test whether the predicted mitochondria are
usable as biological objects. The chapter's main critical point is that none of
these families is sufficient on its own. Dice remains useful, but it is only one
part of a defensible biology-facing evaluation.

\section{Gap Addressed by This Dissertation}

The literature makes three points clear. First, U-Net-style semantic models
remain strong and widely used baselines. Second, nnU-Net provides a powerful
automated alternative to manual configuration. Third, overlap metrics remain the
dominant reporting standard even though prior work already suggests that they
are insufficient for instance-sensitive biological analysis.

What the literature does less well is connect these points in a controlled way.
Many papers either compare specialised models under different supervision setups,
or report semantic scores without asking whether those scores are biologically
sufficient. The missing comparison is not another leaderboard-style benchmark. It is
a controlled test of how a manually designed semantic model and a
self-configuring semantic model compare when they are trained on the same
biological source-level split, evaluated with the same post-processing, and
judged using both voxel-level and object-level metrics on a heterogeneous EM
dataset. This dissertation addresses that gap directly. Its contribution is not
to propose a new segmentation architecture, but to show how the judgement of
model quality changes once biological reliability, rather than overlap alone,
becomes part of the basis for comparison.






\chapter{Implementation}

This chapter describes the experimental pipeline used to compare the custom
U-Net and nnU-Net on CEM-MitoLab. The central design goal was to make the
comparison as controlled as possible: both models should train on the same
split, consume the same preprocessed inputs where feasible, and be evaluated by
the same post-processing and evaluation code.

\section{Overall System Design}

The pipeline has four components: dataset conversion and split definition, a
custom U-Net training stack, an nnU-Net training stack, and a shared
evaluation framework. The dataset contains 21,871 annotated 2D EM slices. In
the resplit workspace used for the final experiments, these are partitioned
into 15,312 training slices, 3,247 validation slices, and 3,312 test slices,
while preserving a source-level split of 415/89/89 biological samples across
train/validation/test. This separation matters because the dissertation is not
only comparing two segmentation models; it is also comparing them under a
controlled data regime intended to remove split-induced inconsistencies.

The shared infrastructure around both models was written specifically for this
project: dataset conversion, split synchronisation, U-Net training and
inference, SLURM submission scripts, and the evaluation code. nnU-Net itself
was installed as an external package and treated as a black-box training
framework. The methodological effort therefore lay in making the surrounding
pipeline consistent and reproducible rather than in modifying nnU-Net's core
implementation.

\section{Data Preparation and Split Alignment}
\label{sec:impl-pipeline}

The raw CEM-MitoLab TIFF images and masks were converted into an nnU-Net-style
dataset named \texttt{Dataset001\_Mitochondria}. Semantic training masks were
derived from the original instance labels by binarising all non-zero
mitochondrion IDs, while the original instance masks were retained separately
for object-level evaluation. The conversion step also writes the custom fold
definition used throughout the project.

\subsection{Format Discovery: \texttt{.npz} to \texttt{.b2nd}}

nnU-Net v2.6 stores preprocessed training tensors as \texttt{.b2nd} files
rather than the older \texttt{.npz} format. The custom U-Net therefore reads
the same \texttt{.b2nd} tensors that nnU-Net trains on, using the
\texttt{blosc2} library to access the arrays on disk. This ensures that both
models see the same nnU-Net-preprocessed, z-score normalised image inputs
during training and validation.

\subsection{Variable Spatial Dimensions and Collation}

The preprocessed slices do not all share the same spatial dimensions, so
PyTorch's default batch collation is insufficient. To preserve the full field
of view, a custom \texttt{pad\_collate\_fn} pads each batch dynamically to the
maximum height and width present in that batch, and the dataset class pads each
sample to a multiple of 32 before it enters the network. This choice avoids any
crop-based asymmetry between the custom U-Net and nnU-Net while still allowing
mini-batch training.

\subsection{Split Alignment}

The canonical split file for the final experiments is
\texttt{splits\_final.json}, stored in both the raw and preprocessed nnU-Net
dataset directories. Fold~0 of this file is synchronised with the project's
\texttt{splits.json} and validated against \texttt{nnunet\_id\_map.json} before
training starts. The custom U-Net loader reads the same fold~0 definition, so
the U-Net and nnU-Net train on identical train/validation cases. The held-out
test set remains separate from this fold definition and is evaluated only after
training, using the same downstream evaluation script for both models.

\section{Custom U-Net}

The custom model is a 2D U-Net implemented in PyTorch from scratch. It follows
the standard encoder-decoder design with skip connections, using four
down-sampling blocks and a bottleneck, with channel widths 64, 128, 256, 512,
and 1024 before the decoder upsamples back to full resolution. Each
\texttt{DoubleConv} block uses two 3$\times$3 convolutions with
\texttt{InstanceNorm2d} and \texttt{ReLU} activations. Instance normalisation
was chosen because the custom model trains with a batch size of 4, for which
batch-normalisation statistics are unstable.

To make the comparison closer to nnU-Net, the U-Net now predicts two classes
(background and mitochondria) and is trained with a combined cross-entropy plus
soft Dice objective. The Dice term is implemented as batch-level softmax Dice
over the foreground channel only, matching nnU-Net's non-region
\texttt{CE + Dice} training path. During training and validation, the reported
Dice is also aligned with nnU-Net: it is computed from epoch-level hard
true-positive, false-positive, and false-negative counts rather than as a mean
of per-image Dice values.

The optimiser is \texttt{AdamW} with learning rate $10^{-3}$, and the learning
rate is reduced on plateau using \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} with patience 3 and
factor 0.5. Training is run for up to 1,000 epochs with validation every second
epoch and early stopping if validation loss fails to improve by at least 0.001
for 50 validation checks. Data augmentation is applied only to the training
split and consists of horizontal and vertical flips, random 90-degree
rotations, small brightness shifts, contrast scaling, and additive Gaussian
noise. The trainer writes both \texttt{best\_model.pt} and
\texttt{last\_checkpoint.pt}; the latter stores the model, optimiser,
scheduler, and loss history so that SLURM preemption does not force a restart
from scratch.

Training is launched through SLURM on the Hastings teaching partition with one
GPU, 32 GB RAM, and two CPU workers. Held-out test inference is performed in a
separate prediction step so that the final scoring of U-Net and nnU-Net is
always done through the same evaluation code path.

\section{nnU-Net Configuration}

nnU-Net was installed as a Python package and used without modification to its
own training logic. For this dataset it selected the 2D configuration, with a
patch size of 512$\times$512 and batch size 12. The resulting network is a
\texttt{PlainConvUNet} with eight stages, feature widths
\{32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 512, 512, 512\}, \texttt{InstanceNorm2d}
normalisation, and \texttt{LeakyReLU} activations. These choices come directly
from nnU-Net's planning stage and are part of what the dissertation is
evaluating: whether automatic configuration can compete with a custom
implementation under the same data split and evaluation framework.

Training is invoked as \texttt{nnUNetv2\_train 001 2d 0 --npz --c}. The
\texttt{--npz} flag preserves probability maps needed for calibration and
threshold-sensitivity analysis, while \texttt{--c} enables resume-from-checkpoint
behaviour under SLURM preemption. The launch script validates that the raw and
preprocessed \texttt{splits\_final.json} files match each other and match the
active project split before training begins, preventing nnU-Net from silently
falling back to its own autogenerated cross-validation folds.

A cluster-specific environment issue did require attention: under Python~3.12,
the local PyTorch 2.10.0+cu128 installation failed inside the inductor backend
because of an invalid type annotation in
\texttt{torch/\_inductor/codegen/cutedsl/cutedsl\_kernel.py}. This was resolved
by patching the PyTorch installation on the cluster, not by editing nnU-Net
itself. From the perspective of the dissertation, nnU-Net therefore remains an
unmodified external baseline.

\section{Evaluation Framework}

Evaluation is performed by a single script shared by both models. For each test
case, the script reads a binary prediction mask, a foreground probability map,
and the original instance-labelled ground truth. The same evaluation code is
used for U-Net and nnU-Net so that metric differences cannot be attributed to
post-processing differences.

\subsection{Voxel-Level Metrics}

Voxel-level metrics are computed at foreground thresholds
\{0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}. At each threshold the evaluation records
foreground Dice, background Dice, mean Dice, IoU, precision, recall, HD95, and
boundary F1 at 2-pixel and 5-pixel tolerance. HD95 is computed from boundary
pixels using Euclidean distance transforms rather than from the full mask.
Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is computed from the probability maps using 15
bins. This produces both a default-threshold comparison at 0.5 and a
threshold-sensitivity analysis showing whether the ranking between models is
stable across operating points.

\subsection{Object-Level Metrics}

Because both models are trained as semantic segmenters, predicted instances are
recovered by connected-component labelling of the binary foreground mask using a
3$\times$3 connectivity structure. These recovered components are compared with
the ground-truth instance masks by constructing a full pairwise IoU matrix and
then applying Hungarian matching for one-to-one assignments. From this matrix,
the evaluation computes object count error, signed count error, merge events,
split events, merge rate, split rate, connectivity error, mean matched IoU,
Panoptic Quality at IoU $> 0.5$, and object-level precision/recall at IoU
thresholds 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9. Merge and split events are derived from the
overlap structure itself: a predicted component overlapping multiple
ground-truth objects indicates a merge, whereas a ground-truth object overlapped
by multiple predicted components indicates a split.

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation with Instance Recovery}

Both models remain semantic segmentation systems throughout training; neither is
given an instance-aware loss or detector head. The object-level behaviour is
therefore assessed by applying the same connected-component post-processing to
both models at evaluation time. This is a deliberate methodological choice. The
question being asked is not whether either system is the best possible instance
segmentation model, but whether standard semantic segmentation outputs are
sufficiently biologically faithful once they are converted into object-level
predictions in the same way for both models.

\subsection{High-Dice Corner Cases}

To test whether strong overlap can still conceal biologically important
mistakes, the evaluation also includes a simple rule-based corner-case
diagnostic. A \emph{high-Dice corner case} is defined as a test slice with
foreground Dice above 0.85 at the default threshold of 0.5, but with either
object count error greater than 4 or split rate greater than 0. This rule is
intentionally conservative and easy to interpret: it targets predictions that
would appear strong under Dice alone, yet still show a substantial counting
error or any topological fragmentation of a ground-truth mitochondrion.

The held-out test set contains 3,312 images drawn from biological sources not

by predicting nothing, the distinction between GT-positive and GT-empty cases is
maintained throughout the analysis. Unless otherwise stated, all aggregate
figures are reported over all 3,312 test images; supplementary GT-positive-only
figures are provided where the difference is diagnostically meaningful.

\subsection{Corner Case Analysis: High Dice Does Not Guarantee Biological Reliability}
\label{sec:nnunet-corner}

A central claim of this dissertation is that high Dice does not guarantee
biologically reliable predictions. To test this directly, the 1,684 test images
on which nnU-Net achieves foreground Dice $>0.85$ (50.8\% of the test set) are
examined for elevated object-level error. Under the rule defined in
Chapter~3---object count error $>4$ or split rate $>0$---289
of the 1,684 high-Dice images (17.2\%) still exhibit at least one elevated
biological metric.

This rate is not negligible. A downstream biologist using Dice $>0.85$ as a
quality gate would accept approximately one in six predictions as reliable when
they are in fact topologically defective. The practical consequence is that
organelle counting or morphology analysis performed on this subset would include
a systematic contamination of incorrectly structured objects, even though the
pixel-level quality appears excellent.

This rule is deliberately conservative. nnU-Net is generally accurate, so a
count error greater than four objects or any split event already marks a slice
as biologically questionable despite its strong Dice score. The corner cases are
therefore not failures of a weak model but genuine topological errors in a
strong one, invisible to the Dice filter.

nnU-Net, but is also less spatially precise at the boundary level.

\paragraph{High-Dice corner cases.}
The central claim of the dissertation is that high Dice does not guarantee
biological reliability. That claim is supported for both models. Using the same
rule-based definition introduced earlier, a \emph{high-Dice corner case} is a
slice with foreground Dice $>0.85$ but either object count error $>4$ or split
rate $>0$. Under this definition, 289 of 1,684 high-Dice nnU-Net cases (17.2\%)
still show elevated object-level error. The U-Net behaves almost identically:
228 of 1,340 high-Dice slices (17.0\%) also fail this criterion. High overlap
therefore provides false reassurance for both architectures, even though
nnU-Net remains the better model overall.

\chapter{Conclusions}

\section{Summary of Findings}

This dissertation asked whether the Dice similarity coefficient is a sufficient
evaluation criterion for mitochondria segmentation in electron microscopy, using
a comparison between a custom U-Net and nnU-Net on CEM-MitoLab as the
experimental vehicle. Both models have now been fully evaluated on the held-out
test set under the corrected, split-aligned evaluation pipeline.

The results support a clear answer to the research question. nnU-Net achieves a
mean foreground Dice of 0.686 and mean PQ of 0.551, outperforming the custom
U-Net, which reaches 0.636 and 0.480 respectively. Yet strong Dice does not
imply biological reliability for either model. Around 17\% of high-Dice
predictions for both architectures still exhibit elevated object-level error
under the corner-case definition used in Chapter~4. Object count error, merge
and split rates, and Panoptic Quality therefore expose failure modes that
pixel-level overlap alone does not capture.

\section{Main Conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Dice similarity coefficient should not be treated as a sufficient
  standalone metric for a biology-facing mitochondria segmentation study,
  because it does not directly encode instance topology or object counting
  behaviour.

  \item A fair comparison between a custom U-Net and nnU-Net requires matched
  split handling, matched preprocessing where feasible, and a shared evaluation
  pipeline. Without that alignment, metric differences are difficult to
  interpret.

  \item Object-level analysis is necessary alongside voxel-level analysis when
  the downstream scientific question depends on organelle identity, count, and
  separation rather than foreground overlap alone.

  \item nnU-Net is the stronger model
  overall. It exceeds the custom U-Net on voxel overlap, boundary quality, and
  object-level precision and recall, although the U-Net misses slightly fewer
  positive slices outright and under-counts objects somewhat less severely on
  average.

  \item High-Dice corner cases are not rare curiosities. Under a simple and
  conservative rule, 17.2\% of high-Dice nnU-Net slices and 17.0\% of
  high-Dice U-Net slices still show elevated object-level error.
\end{enumerate}

The central methodological contribution of this dissertation is a unified,
reproducible evaluation pipeline that makes the metric gap measurable and
interpretable, providing a framework for assessing biologically motivated
metrics alongside Dice in EM segmentation research.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations should be noted. Both models produce binary semantic
predictions; instance behaviour is recovered by connected-component
post-processing rather than instance-aware training, which may not reflect the
performance achievable by end-to-end instance segmentation architectures.
nnU-Net is evaluated using a single fold rather than its default five-fold
ensemble, likely underestimating its full capability. The U-Net was trained with
batch size 4 due to GPU memory constraints, compared to nnU-Net's batch size of
12, which may impede convergence independently of architecture. The comparison
is confined to one dataset; results should not be assumed to generalise beyond
the conditions represented in CEM-MitoLab. In addition, U-Net test inference
had to fall back to raw TIFF images with z-score normalisation because no test
\texttt{.b2nd} tensors were available, introducing a small mismatch between the
U-Net's training-time and test-time input path. Formal paired significance
testing was completed for the final resplit evaluation using Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests with Benjamini--Hochberg correction, but the inferential
analysis is still limited to paired 2D slice-level metrics on a single dataset
and therefore should not be overgeneralised beyond this experimental setting.

Future work could evaluate nnU-Net with its full five-fold ensemble, compare the
current semantic models against genuinely instance-aware alternatives, apply the
evaluation framework to additional EM datasets and model families, and extend
the corner case analysis to 3D volumetric settings where merge and split errors
along the $z$-axis introduce additional complexity.
